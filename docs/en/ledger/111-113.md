# Judgment cards J-109–J-111

[中文版本](../../zh/ledger/111-113.md)

This shard registers the three new cards in C15, “Three Clocks of the Household.”

### J-109 · Housing costs will keep delaying household formation into later life and fewer affordable places

- **Proposed date**: 2026-10-16
- **One-sentence judgment**: From 2026 to 2032, housing-cost pressure in the high-income OECD member-country urban sample will continue to delay first independent residence, cohabitation/marriage, and first births, concentrating family formation in fewer affordable places; housing, childcare, and transport policy can buffer the mechanism but will not return every household to the previous generation’s floor area and geography.
- **Audience scale**: Affected population = tenants, first-time buyers, and families in high-income OECD cities, a hundred-millions-scale constructed impact reach (J-109’s population universe is the comparable OECD-member rows listed by EXT-117 (the city sample is the high-income urban observations with comparable housing indicators within those members, deduplicated by member-country ID, with no addition of member-country and urban-area populations), not an action denominator); actual repeated actors/behaviour denominator = not applicable, because this card judges an institutional relationship between housing costs and family formation rather than one weekly deduplicated social action; capability classification = not applicable (it neither raises existing professionals’ ceiling nor enables people who previously could not act).
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Not applicable: this is an urban and family-institution judgment, not diffusion of a new technology.
- **What can be done now**: A city-housing policy analyst: within two weeks, select three metropolitan areas from the preregistered sample and align price-to-income, rent burden, population inflow, age at first independent residence, and age at first birth in one table; read next (linked to “Leading indicator”): housing burden, permits, net inflow, independent-residence age, and first-birth age by year；Use the three metropolitan-area records in the same table first; national annual data are context only.
- **Lens**: Supply and demand, human needs, historical patterns, and social structure; technology is not the driver.
- **Reasoning chain**: Jobs and education concentrate in cities → households pay higher prices and rents for access → young adults delay independent residence or move family formation farther away → commuting, care, and childcare time rise → the same income must cover housing and children → only housing supply, public childcare, transport, or predictable income can loosen the combined constraint; in-migration first raises competition in scarce places rather than automatically creating affordable homes.
- **Time window**: 2026-01-01 to 2032-12-31
- **Falsifier**: Pre-register “high pressure” as an OECD-comparable price-to-income ratio or rent burden in the top quartile of the target urban sample; “sustained population inflow” as a net inflow rate of at least 0.5% per year for three consecutive years; and “material fall” as a three-year-average fall relative to the 2023–2025 baseline of at least 0.2 years in age at first independent residence or first birth, or 2 percentage points in childlessness, supported by a 95% confidence interval on the same statistical definition. If any urban area lacks the required age, childlessness, or 95% confidence-interval measure, exclude that urban area; if fewer than three complete urban areas remain, record indeterminate rather than treating missingness as non-refutation. If at least three urban areas meeting the first two conditions also meet those fall conditions, the direction that housing stress keeps delaying family formation is refuted; if housing stress falls but family formation does not recover, it refutes housing as the main constraint, not every other constraint.
- **Leading indicator**: OECD price-to-income and rent-burden measures; age at first independent residence, age at first birth, and childlessness; urban housing permits and net population inflow; annually.
- **Confidence**: Medium
- **depends-on**: J-096, J-105
- **Strongest opposing mechanism**: Remote work, rail, or large-scale public housing could provide affordable space without leaving employment centres; alternatively, prices could fall while partnership and fertility do not recover, showing that labour markets and relationship norms are the main constraints.
- **Against consensus**: Agreement: housing burden affects family formation, a common mechanism in OECD social-policy discussion. Divergence: this does not make “expensive housing means fewer births” a single cause, and does not predict every city will move together. Retained because housing is measurable and the urban comparison counter-case is explicit.
- **External comparison source**: EXT-117, EXT-119
- **Source**: [C15 section 2](../chains/150-households-three-clocks.md#2-j-109-housing-costs-will-keep-delaying-household-formation-into-later-life-and-fewer-affordable-places)
- **Next review**: 2027-12-31
- **Status**: ACTIVE

### J-110 · Migration is a labour-and-care shock absorber, not a reversal of population ageing

- **Proposed date**: 2026-10-16
- **One-sentence judgment**: From 2026 to 2032, major OECD receiving countries will continue using permanent and temporary migration to ease labour and care shortages; migration can improve local ratios of working-age people to service demand but cannot restore the old age structure within one generation, and the receiving place’s constraints will move toward housing, credential recognition, language, schools, job quality, and political integration.
- **Audience scale**: Affected population = residents, migrants, and their families in those ten receiving countries, a hundred-millions-scale constructed impact reach (J-110’s population universe is the ten receiving countries with the largest average inflow in the two most recent annual columns of EXT-118’s 2024-edition table, deduplicated by member-country ID, not an action denominator); actual repeated actors/behaviour denominator = not applicable, because this card judges institutional carrying capacity rather than personal action; capability classification = not applicable (it neither raises existing professionals’ ceiling nor enables people who previously could not act).
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Not applicable: migration flows are institutional and demographic indicators, not one repeated consumer action.
- **What can be done now**: A labour-and-care planning lead in a receiving country: within two weeks, choose one of the ten registered major receivers and align permanent or temporary inflow, care vacancies, housing burden, qualification wait, and school capacity by year; read next (linked to “Leading indicator”): working-age net migration, care vacancies and wages, migrant housing burden, and recognition wait；Use the selected receiving country’s annual table first; top-ten receiver totals are context only.
- **Lens**: Supply and demand, population structure, historical patterns, social structure, and institutional constraints; it does not start from AI or abundance → scarcity.
- **Reasoning chain**: Ageing reduces the local supply of new workers → health, care, construction, and service jobs still need people → migration can change the geography of working-age population within years → migrants also need housing, schools, status, and training → the buffer concentrates where carrying and credential systems exist → higher net migration does not restore the old dependency ratio because migrants age too and the required scale accumulates over time.
- **Time window**: 2026-01-01 to 2032-12-31
- **Falsifier**: Pre-register “major OECD receiving countries” as the ten countries with the largest average inflow in the two most recent annual columns of the 2024-edition table, using the table in EXT-118 as the frozen list; “working-age migrant share reaches the registered baseline” means it reaches that country’s 2019 value; and “does not rise” means the care-vacancy rate and housing-burden rate stay within 2 percentage points of their 2019–2025 mean and credential-recognition waiting time stays within 110% of that mean. If at least three of those countries have positive net migration for three consecutive years, reach the working-age baseline, return the old-age dependency ratio to its 2019 level or below, and keep all three carrying indicators within those thresholds, the strong form “migration only buffers, it cannot reverse age structure” is refuted; missing baseline data is indeterminate.
- **Leading indicator**: OECD International Migration Outlook data on permanent and temporary migration, work permits, and family reunification; working-age net migration; care vacancies and wages; migrant housing burden, credential-recognition waiting times, and local school capacity; annually.
- **Confidence**: Medium
- **depends-on**: J-096, J-105, J-107
- **Strongest opposing mechanism**: Sustained large-scale young migration, later retirement, and productivity gains could improve working-age population and dependency ratios more than expected; the opposite is political exclusion that suppresses legal migration and makes care shortages worsen before demographic ratios move.
- **Against consensus**: Agreement: migration can ease labour shortages and receiving countries need integration capacity. Divergence: migration is not treated as the complete solution to ageing, and political reaction is not assumed to be uniformly exclusionary. Retained because age structure, housing, and credential recognition separately test “buffer” versus “reversal.”
- **External comparison source**: EXT-118
- **Source**: [C15 section 3](../chains/150-households-three-clocks.md#3-j-110-migration-is-a-labour-and-care-shock-absorber-not-a-reversal-of-population-ageing)
- **Next review**: 2027-12-31
- **Status**: ACTIVE

### J-111 · Fertility policy is more likely to change timing and gaps than restore replacement fertility across the OECD

- **Proposed date**: 2026-10-16
- **One-sentence judgment**: By 2035, total fertility rates will remain below 2.1 in most OECD countries; the more plausible policy result is to reduce postponement caused by housing, childcare, and work conflicts and to make some countries higher than comparable countries without complementary policy, rather than return the OECD as a whole to replacement fertility.
- **Audience scale**: Affected population = couples of reproductive age, children, and future public-finance contributors in OECD countries, a hundred-millions-scale constructed impact reach (J-111’s population universe is the 38 OECD members in EXT-119’s 2024-edition Family Database table, deduplicated by member-country ID, not an action denominator); actual repeated actors/behaviour denominator = not applicable, because this card judges demographic outcomes and institutional combinations rather than a weekly deduplicated social action; capability classification = not applicable (it neither raises existing professionals’ ceiling nor enables people who previously could not act).
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Not applicable: this is a demographic-outcome and institutional-combination judgment.
- **What can be done now**: A family-policy evaluator: within two weeks, align childcare cost, housing burden, income loss after parental leave, age at first birth, and total fertility rate for three OECD countries in the same years; read next (linked to “Leading indicator”): childcare coverage, first-birth age, childlessness, female employment, and total fertility rate；Use the same-year tables for the three OECD countries first; OECD-wide averages are context only.
- **Lens**: Historical patterns, population structure, supply and demand, human needs, and social institutions; technology is only a possible administrative aid.
- **Reasoning chain**: Longer education and employment paths delay partnership and parenthood → the opportunity and housing costs of children are concentrated into a shorter life stage → a single cash policy covers only part of the price and cannot replace childcare, partnership, or predictable work → France and Nordic combinations show that the gap can be partly compressed; low fertility in Singapore, Japan, and South Korea shows that a single incentive does not automatically restore replacement fertility → the more likely future is modest timing recovery and wider country differences, not a broad OECD rebound.
- **Time window**: 2026-01-01 to 2035-12-31
- **Falsifier**: Pre-register the denominator as the 38 OECD members in the 2024 edition. By 2035, if at least 26 members have total fertility rates at or above 2.1 for three consecutive years, and both an age-standardised series and a native-born/immigrant composition split still rise (rather than the rise being explained only by age structure, migrant composition, or measurement change), the direction that most remain below replacement is refuted. A few countries reaching 2.1 would not refute the majority claim but would require narrowing the institutional-combination boundary; countries without comparable component data do not enter the numerator, and missingness is not counted as success.
- **Leading indicator**: OECD total fertility rates, age at first birth, and childlessness; childcare enrolment and household out-of-pocket cost; housing burden; female employment and income loss after parental leave; annually.
- **Confidence**: Medium
- **depends-on**: J-109, J-110
- **Strongest opposing mechanism**: Housing supply, childcare, and work institutions could improve together, or a new low-cost care arrangement could let many postponed births occur; another opposing path is low-fertility countries expanding migration and changing the statistical denominator, producing a surface rebound.
- **Against consensus**: Agreement: OECD fertility is generally below replacement and policy bundles matter more than slogans. Divergence: “policy is ineffective” and “policy restores replacement” are not the only choices; the judgment predicts limited recovery and wider country differences. Retained because 2.1, three consecutive years, and composition controls provide an explicit expiry test.
- **External comparison source**: EXT-119
- **Source**: [C15 section 4](../chains/150-households-three-clocks.md#4-j-111-fertility-policy-is-more-likely-to-change-timing-and-gaps-than-restore-replacement-fertility-across-the-oecd)
- **Next review**: 2028-12-31
- **Status**: ACTIVE
