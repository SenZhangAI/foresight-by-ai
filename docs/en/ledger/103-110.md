### J-103 · The society-scale AI action is asking, not deciding; hundred-million weekly reach rests on the free tier

- **Proposed date**: 2026-10-10
- **One-sentence judgment**: As of 2026-10-10, “on one’s own initiative, asking a general-purpose AI chat assistant a question or handing it a task” has reached the S tier (a single service self-reports more than 900 million weekly active users; the lower bound is self-reported and unaudited); as long as the free tier exists, it stays above a hundred million people weekly through 2029-10-10; while “handing a purchase or booking to an AI to place and pay for” does not reach a hundred million people weekly in the same window — what has reached society scale is asking and handing off low-stakes tasks, not deciding for people and not paying for them.
- **Audience scale**: The impact reach includes internet users who passively see AI summaries on search pages, a constructed billion-scale reach that is not counted in the denominator; the actual repeated actors are de-duplicated individuals who, at least once in 7 days, on their own initiative ask a general-purpose AI chat assistant a question or hand it a task — a single service’s self-reported lower bound is above 900 million, hundred-millions-scale (the S-tier threshold is 100 million; the lower bound is self-reported and unaudited, and services may not be added together). It mainly lets people who could not do it do it now: more than seven in ten messages are non-work, and the top three uses are practical guidance, seeking information, and writing; it also raises existing professionals' ceiling, but that is not where the scale comes from.
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Gate 1 **PASS**: a single service self-reports more than 900 million weekly active users (2026-02-27, first-hand), nine times the 100-million threshold; evidence strength is “lower bound self-reported, unaudited”; the survey measures (Pew: 24% of US adults use an AI chatbot daily; Reuters Institute: 34% weekly use of generative AI across six countries) give only shares — this round obtained no citable population base, so they give no independent head count. Gate 2 passes when counted per single occasion: one question replaces one web-search query; replacement in total volume is not shown (Apple testimony says Safari searches fell; Google says overall queries are still growing). Gate 3 passes: the user side needs only existing phones and browsers, and inference compute is carried by the provider through subscription, enterprise, and API revenue. Gate 4 passes: a unilateral personal decision with free sign-up. Gate 5 passes on the basis of voluntary repeat use already observed at T; net burden is not directly measured. The L8 demand-side review does not veto, but confines the society-scale action to the “ask / hand off low-stakes tasks” side. Gate-by-gate grounds and gaps: [Verdict and check log §2](../../evidence/social-action-verdict-2026-10-10.en.md#2-gate-by-gate-verdict-on-the-main-candidate).
- **What can be done now**: In product or research work, change the denominator from “how many people will sign off on an AI-generated option” to “how many people come each week to ask or hand something off”; design for the asking side — answers that can be checked, sources that can be traced, errors that can be corrected; treat “ordering and paying on someone’s behalf” as a high-liability channel that needs authorisation, revocation, and someone who carries liability (see J-098), and do not plan delegated-payment products on the premise of hundreds of millions of users; keep tracking free-tier terms and how weekly-active figures are defined, because they are this card’s only non-technical necessary premise.
- **Lens**: Diffusion lag (**L4**: “can be done” is not the same as being adopted; used to turn a direction into a time window — this card’s three-year window and free-tier premise), cost structure (**L3**: the free tier is sustained by cross-subsidy from subscription, enterprise, and API revenue, which is a matter of product bundling), human nature and demand (**L8**: the “certainty” and “accountability” anchors confine the society-scale action to the asking side; this card does not require human nature to change). This card starts from a frozen countable action, not from “what becomes abundant”, and does not invoke **L1** (codes in [Methodology §2](../00-method.md#2-toolbox-of-lenses)).
- **Reasoning chain**: The need to find answers and to get help writing something predates AI, and searching and asking people are its old actions (L8; no change in human nature required) → general-purpose chat assistants enter phones and browsers that are already ubiquitous through a free tier, and adoption needs no one else to change at the same time (Gates 3 and 4) → one question replaces one search or one request to a person, and voluntary repeat use rose through 2023–2026 (self-reported weekly users 100 million → more than 900 million) → but answers can be wrong, and anything with consequences needs someone accountable (L8’s “certainty” and “accountability”) → the society-scale action stops at asking and handing off low-stakes tasks; deciding (J-072) and ordering-and-paying on someone’s behalf need authorisation, revocation, and a liable party (J-098), and neither clears Gate 1 at T → the necessary premise of the scale is the free tier: counting payers only gives a self-reported lower bound above 59 million at T and, at a paying share of about 5 percent, stays in the tens of millions, which would drop the result to the D tier.
- **Time window**: 2026-10-10 to 2029-10-10.
- **Falsifier**: Any one of the following falsifies the corresponding part of this card: (1) before 2029-10-10, any platform publicly reports at least 100 million de-duplicated weekly users who order or pay through an AI on their behalf (overturns “delegated payment does not reach a hundred million weekly in the window”); (2) evidence independent of the providers — an audit, a regulatory disclosure, or a survey with a stated sampling design multiplied by a citable population base — shows that every single service’s de-duplicated weekly denominator was below 100 million at T (overturns “the S tier was reached at T”); (3) within the window, one of the three largest general-purpose chat assistants removes its free tier, and its publicly reported weekly active users are still at least 100 million a year after removal (overturns “the free tier is a necessary premise”; if no service removes its free tier within the window, this item is recorded as undeterminable, not as passed); (4) with the free tier still in place, every qualifying measure falls below 100 million by 2029-10-10 (overturns “it stays at the S tier”).
- **Leading indicator**: Providers’ published weekly active users and consumer-subscriber counts (if a single service’s paying users reach 100 million, the free tier stops being a necessary premise and this card must be rewritten); changes to free-tier terms (quotas, ads, removal); survey shares for “used in the past week” with stated sampling designs, plus citable population bases; whether any platform starts publishing de-duplicated weekly users of AI ordering/payment on someone’s behalf; search-query series independent of the providers; annually.
- **Confidence**: Medium
- **depends-on**: J-001, J-066, J-072, J-098.
- **Strongest opposing mechanism**: A capital pullback: if returns on training and inference investment disappoint, providers narrow or remove the free tier, or load it with ads and strict quotas, and weekly users could fall back to a tens-of-millions paying population — exactly the path along which this card’s necessary premise is deleted. Another opposing mechanism comes from the other direction: operating-system and payment platforms make AI ordering a default entry point (one-tap authorisation, revocable by default), so delegated payment crosses a hundred million weekly within three years, and the part of this card about delegated payment fails.
- **Against consensus**: **Consistent with consensus here**: “ChatGPT is one of the fastest-adopted consumer products, with hundreds of millions of weekly users” is a widely reported claim; this card’s Gate 1 verdict only re-checks it under the frozen measure and is not an independent discovery. **Divergence**: it diverges from the claim that “AI agents will soon shop and order for ordinary people” — at T no platform publishes de-duplicated weekly users of AI ordering, and OpenAI’s Instant Checkout was revamped after failing to take off; this card predicts delegated payment does not reach a hundred million weekly in the window. **Why retain**: the scale comes from asking being low-stakes and free, not from AI making decisions for people; accountability does not shift automatically as capability rises (J-098).
- **External comparison source**: EXT-77, EXT-78, EXT-79, EXT-80, EXT-81, EXT-82.
- **Source**: [C1 section X: count it the other way](../chains/10-generation-becomes-free.md#x-count-it-the-other-way-the-society-scale-action-is-asking-not-choosing); frozen definitions in the [countable social action freeze record](../../evidence/social-action-freeze-2026-10-10.en.md); data and gate-by-gate verdict in the [verdict and check log](../../evidence/social-action-verdict-2026-10-10.en.md).
- **Next review**: 2027-10-10.
- **Status**: ACTIVE

### J-104 · Paid work from home stops at the D tier: tens of millions weekly, a plateau since 2023, and the employer decides

- **Proposed date**: 2026-10-10
- **One-sentence judgment**: As of 2026-10-10, “doing at least part of one’s paid work at one’s own home in the reference week” reaches only the D tier (segment diffusion) under the definitions frozen in advance: the qualifying components of the US, the UK and New Zealand sum to about 48 million (all broad definition; about 66 million with the EU added, whose base was not obtained), short of hundred-million weekly reach, and the action is done by more than half of people at work only within standard breakdowns such as the US computer and mathematical, and business and financial operations, occupation groups; through 2029-10-10 it stays on a plateau — the telework rate among US people at work neither expands above 25% nor falls back below 19%; whether people work at home is decided mainly by the employer, not by the person.
- **Current-scale review**: States only facts that had already happened at T, with no forward claim. Under the [freeze record](../../evidence/non-ai-social-action-freeze-2026-10-10.en.md) it is judged C · D tier: in BLS’s September 2026 reference week, 34.679 million people at work teleworked or worked at home for pay (21.9%, broad definition; wage-and-salary measure 28.433 million); ONS “worked from home in the past seven days” 37% × employment of 34.478 million ≈ 12.76 million (broad definition); New Zealand 898,700 (broad definition); EU “usually work from home” 8.9%, employment base not obtained, not counted in the sum; every other country has no qualifying survey and counts as 0. Even taking the EU’s entire population as the base, the sum would still fall short of 100 million. A country without a qualifying survey counts as 0, so the true global count may be higher — this review says only that under the frozen measure the evidence does not support the S tier.
- **Forward claim**: From 2026-10-10 to 2029-10-10 this action **stays on a plateau**: it neither expands to social scale nor falls back to 2019. The basis is the trend before T — BLS’s telework rate has stayed between 21.5% and 23.8% since 2024, averaging about 22.1% in January–September 2026 (about 22.4% in the same period a year earlier); SWAA’s share of paid working days worked entirely at home eased from about 28.8% in 2023 to about 25.9% in January–September 2026, still more than three times 2019’s 7.15%. The mechanism is two non-technical premises: employers hold the decision right and had already tightened it in 2025, and occupational structure caps the jobs that can be done at home at the white-collar breakdowns. This part has its own falsifiers — (1), (2) and (4) below, kept separate from (3), which corresponds to the “Current-scale review” — and its own leading indicators: the BLS monthly telework rate and telework rates by occupation, the SWAA monthly series, and the office-attendance requirements of major employers and governments.
- **Audience scale**: The impact reach includes commuter transport, office buildings and city-centre commerce, and is not counted in the denominator; the actual repeated actors are people at work who do at least part of their paid work at their own home in the reference week — the qualifying components of the US, the UK and New Zealand sum to about 48 million (broad definition; about 66 million with the EU added), tens-of-millions-scale, and the action is done by more than half of people at work within US standard breakdowns such as computer and mathematical, business and financial operations, and professional and technical services. It mainly neither raises existing professionals' ceiling nor lets people who could not do it do it now; it changes where people already employed do their work; the size of the small part that may belong to “lets people who could not do it do it now” (the telework rate of people at work with a disability is 24.4%, above the overall rate) was not measured.
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Gate 1 **FAIL**: the qualifying components sum to about 48.34 million, about 66 million with the EU added, all broad definition, in the tens of millions; both routes to the D tier (tens-of-millions scale, above half within a standard breakdown) hold at once. Gate 2 passes when counted per single occasion, and replacement in total volume is supported by official series: commuting trips per person in England were 112 in 2025, about 20% fewer than in 2019; the US share usually working from home was 5.7% in 2019 and 13.3% in 2024. Gate 3 passes: home broadband and personal devices already exist for other reasons. Gate 4 passes in part: the person cannot decide alone, and the decision lies with the employer; inside each employer it is a local closed loop, and in 2025 the share of federal employees working hybrid fell from 61% to 28%, showing that employers can take it back and already have — this is a constraint with empirical support, but Gate 1 stops the action as well, so it cannot be cited to claim that Gate 4 has independent veto power. Gate 5 passes: relative to commuting to the workplace, voluntary repeat use had already happened at T, and workers value working from home as equivalent to a pay rise of about 8%. The L8 demand-side review does not veto; “the sense of presence” places the equilibrium at hybrid working. The five controls that must be vetoed (remittances, unpaid care, platform gig work, the four-day week, cross-border remote work) were all vetoed at Gate 1, as expected at the freeze. Gate-by-gate grounds and gaps: [Verdict and check log §2](../../evidence/non-ai-social-action-verdict-2026-10-10.en.md#2-gate-by-gate-verdict-on-the-main-candidate).
- **What can be done now**: When building products, services or research for remote or hybrid work, describe the target population as “above half within US white-collar occupation breakdowns, about one in five of all people at work, tens of millions across three countries”, not as “every worker at home”; when planning cities, transport and office buildings, estimate on the basis of “somewhat fewer commutes each week”, not “commuting disappears”; track the office-attendance-day requirements of major employers and governments as the first leading indicator, because they decide how large this market is sooner than any collaboration tool does; if global scale is to be discussed, first fill in qualifying weekly surveys country by country, rather than filling the gaps with platforms’ self-reported numbers.
- **Lens**: Human nature and demand (**L8**: the “sense of presence” and “real relationships” anchors keep the equilibrium at hybrid working; this card does not require human nature to change), cost structure (**L3**: employers set office days by the costs of coordination and supervision, so the decision lies with the organisation, not the individual), diffusion lag (**L4**: used to write the plateau as a time window). This card starts from a frozen countable action, not from “what becomes abundant”, and does not invoke **L1** (codes in [Methodology §2](../00-method.md#2-toolbox-of-lenses)).
- **Reasoning chain**: The demand to commute less and arrange one’s own time has long existed (L8; no change in human nature required) → home broadband and personal devices had long since become common for other reasons (Gate 3), and the forced shutdowns of 2020 gave employers their first large-scale test of working from home → workers value working from home as equivalent to a pay rise of about 8%, and voluntary repeat use held steady through 2023–2026 at more than three times the 2019 level (Gate 5) → but working from home needs the employer’s consent (Gate 4), and about four in ten people at work are in occupations that require being on site — service, construction, production, transportation — with telework rates of only 0.9%–12.8% (occupational structure) → so the scale stops at the tens of millions and is above half only in white-collar breakdowns (Gate 1 fails, D tier) → from 2025 the federal government and large employers tightened office-attendance requirements, and the telework rate moved sideways between 21.5% and 23.8% → within the window it stays on a plateau. AI is not part of this mechanism: delete AI and the conclusion holds unchanged.
- **Time window**: 2026-10-10 to 2029-10-10.
- **Falsifier**: Any one of the following falsifies the corresponding part of this card: (1) over the most recent 12 months before 2029-10-10, the average telework rate in BLS’s Current Population Survey (people-at-work measure, Table A-41) is above 25% or below 19% (overturns “plateau”: the former is expansion, the latter contraction); (2) in any 12 consecutive months within the window, the average telework rate of BLS’s “computer and mathematical occupations” falls below 50% (overturns the continuation of the D-tier route “above half within a standard breakdown”); (3) a qualifying weekly survey with a reference period between 2023-01-01 and 2026-10-10 appears that brings the sum of qualifying components under the frozen rules to ≥ 100 million (overturns the C verdict of the “Current-scale review” — this is an overturn by measurement coverage, not behavioural expansion, and must be recorded separately from (1)); (4) within the window the US or the UK legislates to let workers decide unilaterally to work from home (the employer may not refuse on business grounds), and one year after it takes effect that country’s telework share has still not risen out of its previous range (overturns “the employer’s decision right is a necessary premise”; if no such legislation occurs within the window, this item is recorded as undeterminable, not as passed).
- **Leading indicator**: The BLS Table A-41 monthly telework rate and the Table A-42 telework rates by occupation and by industry; the SWAA monthly series of the share of paid working days worked entirely at home; commuting trips per person in England's National Travel Survey; the ONS share who “worked from home in the past seven days”; the office-attendance-day requirements of major employers and governments and their enforcement; flexible-working legislation; whether national statistical agencies start publishing qualifying weekly working-from-home figures (Canada, Japan, China, India), and the EU employment base; annually.
- **Confidence**: Medium
- **depends-on**: J-066, J-067, J-068, J-069, J-070, J-071.
- **Strongest opposing mechanism**: The white-collar tasks that can be done remotely are exactly the part most easily replaced by AI: if employment in these jobs contracts markedly within the window, the number of teleworkers falls below the lower edge of the range and the plateau ends in contraction — AI appears only in this opposing mechanism, not in this card’s mechanism; delete it and the card’s conclusion still holds. The second opposing mechanism comes from the employer side: an economic downturn raises employers’ bargaining power, office-attendance requirements spread from large employers to small and medium ones, and the telework rate falls below 19%. The third comes from the opposite direction: labour shortages and population ageing lead employers to use working from home as a recruiting chip, and the telework rate rises above 25%.
- **Against consensus**: **Consistent with consensus here**: “remote work entered a plateau after 2023, and hybrid work is the new equilibrium” is a common claim among labour economists and in the business press; this card’s current-scale review only recounts it under the frozen measure and is not an independent discovery. **Divergence**: it diverges from the claim that remote work “has permanently rewritten how most people work” — under the frozen measure it reaches only the tens of millions and is above half only in white-collar breakdowns; this card also points out that, under the frozen measure, the global scale is capped by the coverage of national surveys, a point that is often overlooked. **Why retain**: whether it stays or goes is decided by employers, not by technology or personal preference; the falsifiers for the plateau are written as a numerical range and can be checked when due.
- **External comparison source**: EXT-83, EXT-84, EXT-85, EXT-86, EXT-87, EXT-88.
- **Source**: Not attached to any reasoning-chain text; frozen definitions in the [freeze record for countable social actions that do not presuppose AI](../../evidence/non-ai-social-action-freeze-2026-10-10.en.md); data, gate-by-gate verdict, the five controls and the gap list in the [verdict and check log](../../evidence/non-ai-social-action-verdict-2026-10-10.en.md).
- **Next review**: 2027-10-10.
- **Status**: ACTIVE

### J-105 · Under fiscal squeeze, current recipients' nominal pensions are the hardest to cut; the knife falls first on those not yet drawing

- **Proposed date**: 2026-10-10
- **One-sentence judgment**: From 2026-10-10 to 2034-12-31, no G7 country that can still borrow on the market and is not in a creditor assistance programme passes new legislation that lowers the nominal amount of the median current recipient's basic public pension; the US Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) trust fund is projected to be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032, this gap will not be resolved in a way that leaves people already drawing with less than the amount due under law, and at least half of the long-term benefit-side savings of any fix come from people who have not yet started drawing at the time of enactment.
- **Audience scale**: The impact reach includes current recipients of public pensions in the G7 and working contributors who are not yet drawing, a constructed hundred-millions-scale reach (estimated from each country's population structure; head counts were not verified country by country this round), not counted in the denominator of any weekly action; this card judges legislative and budget behaviour, not an individual action, and the direct deciders are the legislatures and governments of the seven countries. It mainly neither raises existing professionals' ceiling nor lets people who could not do it do it now; it judges whether an income already promised will be cut in nominal terms, and on which generation the burden of a cut falls.
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Gate 1 **FAIL**: this card judges legislative and budget behaviour; there is no individual action that can be de-duplicated and counted weekly, so the society-scale measure does not apply, and it is written as an institutional judgment; Gates 2 to 5 test the diffusion of an action and do not apply to legislative behaviour, so this card does not assess them.
- **Independence test**: Delete J-001 and every card premised on AI/compute, and this card **still holds**: the mechanism uses only the three bills (interest, ageing and defence), the structure of recipients, and the legislative record of 1983–2026; no step presupposes any AI capability.
- **What can be done now**: In pension finance, insurance or public-policy research, treat “current recipients' nominal amounts cut by new legislation” as a low-probability event, and treat later pension ages, threshold freezes, taxation of benefits and changes to indexation formulas as the main sources of risk; when designing savings and insurance products for people aged 40–60 today who are not yet drawing, assume that their pension age will rise and their replacement rate will fall; track how the US Congress handles the OASI gap before 2032 as this card's hardest due check.
- **Lens**: This card does not invoke **L10** (constituency lock-in): its unit is a whole public arrangement, and the pension, as an arrangement, has people who draw it every month; its own calibration case, the 2005 US Social Security personal-accounts proposal, touched only cohorts under 55, yet it is still coded “has defenders”, and it failed. So the step “raising the pension age and changing future formulas falls on cohorts not yet drawing, and is therefore the first cut within reach” is **carried by no lens in L1–L10**, and rests only on the narrative precedents in C13 section 2; the step “current recipients' nominal amounts are the hardest to cut” is **carried by no lens in L1–L10** (L10's own text says only that when such a group exists, outright repeal will meet defenders, “not that the arrangement will survive”); the asymmetry in which nominal cuts are visible and real erosion is not is **carried by no lens in L1–L10 (the human-nature clause “loss aversion” has a source sentence)**; **L1** is not invoked (codes in [Methodology §2](../00-method.md#2-toolbox-of-lenses)).
- **Reasoning chain**: Three bills — interest, ageing and defence — land on the same budget at once (US net interest exceeded defence in fiscal 2024 and 2025; OASI depleted in Q4 2032; NATO core defence of 3.5% by 2035) → tax increases have a ceiling, so some spending must give up share → raising the pension age and changing future formulas falls on cohorts not yet drawing, and is the most common cut in the precedents (carried by no lens: L10's unit is the whole arrangement, and it does not judge sub-cohorts inside a pension; the precedents are the US in 1983, France in 2023 and China in 2024; Canada announced it in its 2012 budget and reversed it in 2016, and the 2005 US personal-accounts proposal, which touched only people under 55, still failed, which shows it is within reach but does not necessarily stick) → for current recipients, countries that can finance themselves switch to methods that never appear in a notification letter: threshold freezes (UK 2022), taxation of benefits and delayed adjustments (US 1983), and automatic formulas written before T (Japan, fiscal 2022, nominal −0.4%); freezes proposed by governments also get deleted by parliaments (France 2026) — a nominal cut needs a visible vote (carried by no lens; the human-nature clause “loss aversion” has a source sentence) → nominal cuts by new legislation appear only in countries that have lost market financing and entered a creditor programme (Greece, Portugal; Ireland, also in a programme, did not cut the weekly rate of the State Pension in its 2011 budget either), or target only high pensions (Italy 2019) → so within the window, the US OASI gap will be resolved without cutting what current recipients are actually paid, and at least half of the long-term benefit-side savings of the fix come from people not yet drawing (carried by no lens, which is why confidence is medium).
- **Time window**: 2026-10-10 to 2034-12-31.
- **Falsifier**: Any one of the following falsifies the corresponding part of this card: (1) within the window, any G7 country (the US, Japan, Germany, the UK, France, Italy, Canada) that, at the time of legislating, can borrow on the market and is not in a creditor assistance programme passes new legislation that brings the monthly nominal amount of the median current recipient's basic public pension below the previous period — an indexation formula written in by new legislation and triggered within the window also counts; written in advance as **not counting**: automatic rules already in force at T (the Japan fiscal 2022 kind), cuts that apply only to high pensions (the Italy 2019 kind), and tax changes; (2) within the window, US Social Security retirement beneficiaries are paid less than the amount due under law, or are paid late, because a trust fund is depleted — congressional **inaction** counts too; (3) a Social Security solvency bill passed in the US within the window contains a provision cutting the nominal monthly benefits of people already drawing; (4) for a solvency bill passed in the US within the window, the scoring by the Social Security Administration's Chief Actuary shows that more than half of the long-term benefit-side savings come from people already drawing at the time of enactment (if the scoring has no such breakdown, this item is recorded as undeterminable, not as passed). At the due check, it must be recorded truthfully whether any G7 member lost market financing or entered a creditor programme within the window; this exclusion may not be used as an excuse after the fact.
- **Leading indicator**: The depletion year and payable share in the annual US Social Security Trustees Report; solvency bills introduced in Congress and the Chief Actuary's scoring; pension indexation provisions and freeze proposals in the annual budgets of the seven countries; each country's long-term government bond yields and sovereign rating actions; whether any G7 member applies for external assistance; annually.
- **Confidence**: Medium
- **depends-on**: —
- **Strongest opposing mechanism**: The bond market can rewrite the order overnight: if a large country suddenly loses market financing within the window, it lands on the Greek and Portuguese side, and nominal cuts come within reach — this card writes that case as an exclusion, so in the most dangerous situation it makes no judgment, and that is its cost. The second opposing mechanism is US deadlock: Congress does nothing before 2032, the Antideficiency Act leaves the Social Security Administration without authority to pay in full and on time, and what current recipients actually receive is held to about 78% by the income limit (the law writes no automatic cut rule; it only forbids paying more than the trust fund holds, 78% is what income can pay, and recipients keep their legal entitlement to full benefits) — this is exactly falsifier (2); this card judges that it will not happen, but no lens carries that judgment.
- **Against consensus**: **Consistent with consensus here**: “current recipients' pensions are politically almost untouchable, and reform can only apply new rules to new entrants” is a common claim in political science and the media. **Divergence**: this card does not accept the strong form of that claim — AFDC in 1996, Greece and Portugal, and Japan in fiscal 2022 all show that current recipients can be cut; this card judges only that the seven countries that can finance themselves will not cut **nominal** amounts by **new legislation**, and it writes the exceptions in advance as exclusions. **Why retain**: nominal cuts and real erosion are asymmetric in visibility, and that decides where the knife falls; but no lens in this project carries that step, so confidence is no higher than medium.
- **External comparison source**: EXT-89, EXT-90, EXT-91, EXT-92, EXT-93, EXT-94, EXT-95, EXT-103, EXT-104, EXT-105, EXT-106, EXT-107, EXT-108, EXT-109, EXT-110, EXT-111, EXT-112, EXT-114.
- **Source**: [C13 section 3](../chains/130-fiscal-squeeze-who-gets-cut.md#3-j-105-current-recipients-nominal-pensions-are-the-hardest-to-cut); historical precedents in [C13 section 2](../chains/130-fiscal-squeeze-who-gets-cut.md#2-history-where-the-knife-fell-and-where-it-did-not); L10's calibration in [Retrospect section 13](../01-retrospect.md#13-the-first-calibration-of-social-regularities-who-draws-the-benefit-and-who-pays-the-bill-2026-10-10).
- **Next review**: 2027-06-30.

### J-106 · Aid gives way to defence: the budget with no domestic periodic recipients is squeezed out first

- **Proposed date**: 2026-10-10
- **One-sentence judgment**: Checked against 2028 data published in 2029, official development assistance (ODA) as a share of gross national income in the UK, France and Germany is in each case still below its own 2023 level (0.58%, 0.50%, 0.79%), and core defence spending as a share of GDP is in each case still above its own 2023 level (NATO 2026 edition: 2.22%, 1.88%, 1.60%); the ODA shares of the UK and Italy are both no higher than 0.35% — the budget with no domestic periodic recipients is squeezed out first, and it does not swing back within the window.
- **Audience scale**: The impact reach is the people in recipient countries who depend on aid programmes, a constructed hundred-millions-scale reach (head counts not verified this round); they do not vote in the donor countries and are not counted in any denominator; this card judges the budget behaviour of four countries, not an individual action, and the direct deciders are the four countries' governments and parliaments. It mainly neither raises existing professionals' ceiling nor lets people who could not do it do it now; it judges whether a line of foreign spending gives way to defence.
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Gate 1 **FAIL**: this card judges budget behaviour; there is no individual action that can be de-duplicated and counted weekly, so it is written as an institutional judgment; Gates 2 to 5 test the diffusion of an action and do not apply to budget behaviour, so this card does not assess them.
- **Independence test**: Delete J-001 and every card premised on AI/compute, and this card **still holds**: the mechanism uses only NATO's signed budget requirement, the beneficiary structure of aid, and the budget record of 2020–2025.
- **What can be done now**: NGOs that depend on European official aid, and health and education programmes in recipient countries, should plan budgets and financing on the basis that aid “will not return to 2023 levels by 2029”, and treat private foundations, multilateral development banks and recipient countries' own public finances as alternative sources; researchers should track separately the share of ODA spent on refugees inside donor countries (13.2% across the DAC in 2025), because it makes ODA figures overstate the money that actually reaches recipient countries.

Italy's ODA was low to begin with (0.27% in 2023) and rose slightly in 2025; this card judges only that it does not exceed 0.35%.
- **Time window**: 2026-10-10 to 2029-12-31.
- **Falsifier**: Any one of the following falsifies this card: (1) the latest OECD data for 2028 available before 2029-12-31 (grant-equivalent measure) show that the ODA share of gross national income of any of the UK, France or Germany is not below its 2023 level; (2) the UK's 2028 ODA share is above 0.35%; (3) Italy's 2028 ODA share is above 0.35%; (4) data from the same NATO edition show that the 2028 core defence share of any of the UK, France or Germany is not above its 2023 level — if defence also falls back, the mechanism “aid gives way to defence” does not hold, even if aid stays low. ### J-107 · Paid weekly work at 65+ stops at the D tier: tens of millions, short of a hundred million

- **Proposed date**: 2026-10-10
- **One-sentence judgment**: As of 2026-10-10, “aged 65 or over and working at least one hour for pay in the reference week” reaches only the D tier (segment diffusion) under the definitions frozen in advance: the components of the seven countries that can be counted sum to about 51.64 million before unpaid family helpers are subtracted, which is an upper bound on the qualifying sum; the subtraction the freeze requires was not carried out this round, and the lower bound derived from Japan and South Korea is about 10.29 million, so the qualifying sum lies in the tens of millions, short of hundred-million weekly reach (the EU, China, Brazil, Indonesia and Mexico count as 0 under the rules); through 2030-10-10, the sum for the same seven-country panel rises but stays below 100 million, and the employment rate of Japan's 65–69 group, on the published figure, stays at 50% or above.
- **Current-scale review**: States only facts that had already happened at T, with no forward claim. Under the [freeze record](../../evidence/older-workers-freeze-2026-10-10.en.md) it is judged **C · D tier only**: the United States 11.374 million (BLS 2025 annual average), Japan 9.43 million (2025), India about 23.15 million (PLFS 2025 current-weekly-status employment rate of 26.2% × the same agency's 65+ population of 88.35 million), South Korea 4.147 million, the United Kingdom 1.654 million, Canada 1.13 million, Australia 758,000, a sum of about 51.64 million; for the EU no qualifying figure with a reference period from 2023 onward was obtained, China publishes only age-composition shares, Brazil's highest age group is 60 and over, Indonesia's figure was not obtained, and Mexico's about 3.5 million was seen only as relayed by the media, while the freeze has no “provisional” tier, so under the rules they all count as 0. **The execution gap on item 6 of the freeze record**: the freeze says “subtract them where the survey reports them separately. Where it does not, mark the component ‘broad definition’”; the surveys of the US, Japan and South Korea are confirmed to report unpaid family helpers separately, the other four countries were not verified and are treated strictly as requiring the subtraction, and this round obtained no country's separate 65+ count, so the subtraction was not carried out. So the about 51.64 million is not the qualifying sum but only its upper bound — subtracting can only lower it, so A is robustly excluded. The lower bound takes Japan's 9.43 million minus the 2.39 million “self-employed workers and family workers” combined (at least 7.04 million), plus South Korea's 4.147 million minus the 899,000 unpaid family workers of all ages in 2023 (about 3.25 million; cross-year, an inference): about 10.29 million; the non-helper part of the US component could only raise the lower bound, but this round has no separate count for it, so it is not counted. The sum route to the D tier (≥ 10 million) holds on this lower bound, with a thin margin, and the South Korea item in it is unverified. The published figure of 54.5% for Japan's 65–69 group includes family workers; whether the group is above half after subtracting them is **not verified**, and it does not carry the verdict. **This verdict holds only under the frozen measure**: China has about 99 million employed people aged 60+ (a second-hand source, a share multiplied by a total, not a form the freeze recognises); once a qualifying 65+ figure appears, the sum could cross 100 million.
- **Forward claim**: From 2026-10-10 to 2030-10-10, the sum for the same seven countries and the same series (a fixed panel) **rises but stays below 100 million**, and the employment rate of Japan's 65–69 group stays at 50% or above every year. The basis is two non-technical premises: legislated increases in the pension age are taking effect (the US full retirement age is 67 for people born in 1960 or later; the UK State Pension age rises from 66 to 67 over 2026–2028; China's gradual increase began in 2025), and the 65+ population itself is growing. This part has its own falsifiers — (1), (2) and (3) below, kept separate from (4), which corresponds to the “Current-scale review” — and its own leading indicators.
- **Audience scale**: The impact reach includes the 65+ populations of the G7, China, India and other countries, a constructed hundred-millions-scale reach that is not counted in the denominator; the actual repeated actors are people aged 65+ who worked at least one hour for pay in the reference week — under the frozen measure the qualifying sum lies between about 10.29 million and 51.64 million, tens-of-millions-scale; Japan's 65–69 group is above half on the published figure, unverified after family workers are subtracted. It mainly neither raises existing professionals' ceiling nor lets people who could not do it do it now; it lets people who are already working delay their exit.
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Gate 1 **FAIL**: the qualifying sum is unknown (the subtraction of unpaid family helpers was not carried out), with a range of about 10.29 million to 51.64 million, in the tens of millions; the sum route to the D tier (≥ 10 million) holds on the lower bound, and the route of above half within a standard age group is unverified. Under the frozen rules, when the sum does not reach 100 million, Gates 2 to 5 and the L8 demand-side review are not evaluated; the verdict records them as “not evaluated”, not as passed. Country-by-country definitions and gaps: [Verdict and check log §2](../../evidence/older-workers-verdict-2026-10-10.en.md#2-gate-by-gate-verdict).
- **Independence test**: Delete J-001 and every card premised on AI/compute, and this card **still holds**: the denominator comes from national labour force surveys, and the forward claim rests on legislated pension ages and population structure; no step presupposes any AI capability.
- **What can be done now**: When building products, recruitment or policy for older workers, describe the target population as “tens of millions across seven countries, with an upper bound of about fifty million”, not as “older people are generally at work”; in countries where the pension age has just risen (the UK in 2026–2028, China from 2025), use the employment rate of the one or two age groups just past the old pension age as the earliest observation point; if global scale is to be discussed, first fill in qualifying counts of employed people aged 65+ for China and the EU, rather than filling the gap by multiplying a share by a total.
- **Lens**: This card does not invoke **L10**: the step “raising the pension age falls on cohorts not yet drawing, and is therefore within reach” is **carried by no lens in L1–L10** (L10's unit is a whole public arrangement, and it does not judge sub-cohorts inside a pension; see the Lens field of [J-105](#j-105--under-fiscal-squeeze-current-recipients-nominal-pensions-are-the-hardest-to-cut-the-knife-falls-first-on-those-not-yet-drawing)); the step “a later pension age makes more people keep working after 65” is **carried by no lens in L1–L10** (it is a labour-supply response to income rules, and the mapping table has no lens that carries it); this card starts from a frozen countable action, not from “what becomes abundant”, and does not invoke **L1** (codes in [Methodology §2](../00-method.md#2-toolbox-of-lenses)).
