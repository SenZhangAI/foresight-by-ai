# C15 · Three Clocks of the Household: How Housing, Migration, and Fertility Rework Society

> **This chain is not a one-cause story in which low fertility mechanically produces one future.** It places three observable but interacting constraints together: housing determines whether a household can form in a desired place; migration changes the geography of labour and care supply; fertility turns today's choices into an age structure twenty or thirty years later. AI is not a premise of this chain. It may change information, design, and execution costs, but it cannot replace the hard constraints of housing, borders, care, and time.

**Lenses not used**: none — the current cards declare no L1–L9 carrier; update this line whenever lens attribution changes.

## 1. Return to history first: the same policy produces different results under different household constraints

Housing, family policy, and population movement cannot be explained by a rule that says “subsidies make people have more children.” Historical material gives at least two success/failure contrasts:

- **Relatively successful combinations**: France has combined cash, childcare, leave, and tax measures over time; Nordic countries institutionalised compatibility between childcare and mothers' employment. They did not restore post-war fertility, but their long-run fertility rates have generally been higher than in low-fertility East Asian countries. This shows that institutions can move part of the family cost away from households; the cost is not necessarily fixed.
- **Failed single-point interventions**: Singapore has repeatedly introduced pronatalist policies yet has remained low-fertility; Japan expanded childcare without automatically returning to replacement fertility. When housing prices, gendered labour-market arrangements, long hours, and delayed partnership do not loosen together, a single payment or slogan rarely rewrites family formation.
- **Pre-registered four-case comparison**: To avoid selecting only successes, this chain pairs France with Japan and Sweden with Singapore as two contrasts in direction. The success-side rule is a total fertility rate above the OECD bottom quartile in most years from 2010 to 2022, alongside a combined childcare/leave/employment-compatibility policy package. The failure-side rule is a total fertility rate below 1.5 for five consecutive years in the same window despite an explicit pronatalist or childcare-expansion policy. This is a screening rule, not causal proof; the country comparison is checked against the OECD series in EXT-119, and housing and migration mechanisms cannot be extrapolated from these four countries alone.
- **Migration has two sides**: OECD migration material shows that migration can add working-age people and ease some shortages in the short run, but receiving places must also provide housing, schools, status, and political integration. Migration is not a machine that restores the age structure; it moves pressure toward carrying capacity and integration.
- **Historical rule**: policies that durably change family formation usually change who carries time costs, who carries housing costs, and who can obtain predictable status after moving. One-off price changes more often change timing than total births.

This chain therefore does not make “abundance → scarcity” the master explanation. It asks it as an optional question: when housing, care, or migrant supply becomes more abundant, does scarcity move to place, status, time, and predictable relationship commitments?

## 2. J-109: Housing costs will keep delaying household formation into later life and fewer affordable places

Housing is not only an asset price. It is a common entry point to employment, education, care, and partner choice. The OECD housing database provides comparable measures of price-to-income ratios, rents, and housing burden. That supports housing stress as one constraint on family formation, but cannot by itself prove that house prices cause every cross-country birth-rate difference.

- **Judgment**: in 2026–2032, in the high-income OECD member-country urban sample, housing-cost pressure will continue to delay first independent residence, cohabitation/marriage, and first births; places with concentrated jobs and population will split into “more opportunity, less affordable family space.” Expanded rental supply, public housing, or childcare can buffer the mechanism, but will not return every household to the previous generation's floor area and geography.
- **Audience scale**: The affected population is tenants, first-time buyers, and families in high-income OECD cities, a hundred-millions-scale constructed impact reach (J-109’s population universe is the comparable OECD-member rows listed by EXT-117 (deduplicated by member-country ID in that database download, with no addition of member-country and urban-area populations), not an action denominator); actual repeated actors/behaviour denominator = not applicable. Direct actors are households, local governments, landlords, and employers; this is not one weekly deduplicated social action. It is an urban and family-institution judgment, not a claim of billion-scale diffusion.
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Not applicable; this is an institutional judgment about housing cost and family formation, not diffusion of a new technology.
- **Lens**: Supply and demand, social structure, human needs, and historical patterns. Technology is not the driver.
- **Reasoning chain**: Jobs and education concentrate in cities → households pay higher rents and prices for access → young adults delay independent residence or move family formation farther away → commuting, care, and childcare time rise → the same income must cover both housing and children → only more housing supply, public childcare, transport, or predictable income can loosen the combined constraint. In-migration first raises competition in scarce places; it does not automatically create affordable homes.
- **Time window**: 2026-01-01 to 2032-12-31.
- **Falsifier**: Pre-register “high pressure” as an OECD-comparable price-to-income ratio or rent burden in the top quartile of the target urban sample; “sustained population inflow” as a net inflow rate of at least 0.5% per year for three consecutive years; and “material fall” as a three-year-average fall relative to the 2023–2025 baseline of at least 0.2 years in age at first independent residence or first birth, or 2 percentage points in childlessness, supported by a 95% confidence interval on the same statistical definition. If any urban area lacks the required age, childlessness, or 95% confidence-interval measure, exclude that urban area; if fewer than three complete urban areas remain, record indeterminate rather than treating missingness as non-refutation. If at least three urban areas meeting the first two conditions also meet those fall conditions, the direction that housing stress keeps delaying family formation is refuted; if housing stress falls but family formation does not recover, it refutes housing as the main constraint, not every other constraint.
- **Leading indicators**: OECD price-to-income and rent-burden measures; age at first independent residence and first birth; childlessness; urban housing permits and net population inflow; annually.
- **Confidence**: Medium
- **depends-on**: J-096, J-105
- **Strongest opposing mechanism**: Remote work, rail, or large-scale public housing could provide affordable space without leaving employment centres; alternatively, prices could fall while partnership and fertility do not recover, showing that labour markets and relationship norms are the main constraints.
- **Against consensus**: Agreement: housing burden affects family formation, a common mechanism in OECD social-policy discussion. Divergence: this does not turn “expensive housing means fewer births” into a single cause, and does not predict every city will move together. Retained because housing is a measurable shared constraint and the city-level counter-case is explicit.

## 3. J-110: Migration is a labour-and-care shock absorber, not a reversal of population ageing

- **Judgment**: Between 2026 and 2032, major OECD receiving countries will continue using permanent and temporary migration to ease labour and care shortages. Migration can improve local ratios of working-age people to service demand, but cannot restore the old age structure within one generation. The receiving place's binding constraint will move from “is anyone coming?” to housing, credential recognition, language, schools, care-job quality, and political integration.
- **Audience scale**: The affected population is residents, migrants, and their families in those ten receiving countries, a hundred-millions-scale constructed impact reach (J-110’s population universe is the ten receiving countries with the largest average inflow in the two most recent annual columns of EXT-118’s 2024-edition table, deduplicated by member-country ID, not an action denominator); actual repeated actors/behaviour denominator = not applicable. Direct decision-makers are national governments, employers, local governments, and professional regulators. This is an institutional judgment, not a claim of billion-scale personal-action diffusion.
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Not applicable; migration flows are institutional and demographic measures, not one repeated consumer action.
- **Lens**: Supply and demand, population structure, historical patterns, social structure, and institutional constraints; the chain does not start from AI or abundance → scarcity.
- **Reasoning chain**: Ageing reduces the local supply of new workers → health, care, construction, and service jobs still need people → migration can change the geography of working-age population within years → migrants also need housing, schools, status, and training → the buffer concentrates where carrying and credential systems exist → higher net migration does not restore the old dependency ratio, because migrants age too and the required scale accumulates over time.
- **Time window**: 2026-01-01 to 2032-12-31.
- **Falsifier**: Pre-register “major OECD receiving countries” as the ten countries with the largest average inflow in the two most recent annual columns of the 2024-edition table, using the table in EXT-118 as the frozen list; “working-age migrant share reaches the registered baseline” means it reaches that country’s 2019 value; and “does not rise” means the care-vacancy rate and housing-burden rate stay within 2 percentage points of their 2019–2025 mean and credential-recognition waiting time stays within 110% of that mean. If at least three of those countries have positive net migration for three consecutive years, reach the working-age baseline, return the old-age dependency ratio to its 2019 level or below, and keep all three carrying indicators within those thresholds, the strong form “migration only buffers, it cannot reverse age structure” is refuted; missing baseline data is indeterminate.
- **Leading indicators**: OECD International Migration Outlook data on permanent and temporary migration, work permits, and family reunification; working-age net migration; care vacancies and wages; migrant housing burden, credential-recognition waiting times, and local school capacity; annually.
- **Confidence**: Medium
- **depends-on**: J-096, J-105, J-107
- **Strongest opposing mechanism**: Sustained large-scale young migration, later retirement, and productivity gains could improve working-age population and dependency ratios more than expected; the opposite is political exclusion that suppresses legal migration and makes care shortages worsen before demographic ratios move.
- **Against consensus**: Agreement: migration can ease labour shortages and receiving countries need integration capacity. Divergence: migration is not treated as the complete solution to ageing, and political reaction is not assumed to be uniformly exclusionary. Retained because age structure, housing, and credential recognition separately test “buffer” versus “reversal.”

## 4. J-111: Fertility policy is more likely to change timing and gaps than restore replacement fertility across the OECD

- **Judgment**: By 2035, total fertility rates will remain below 2.1 in most OECD countries. The more plausible policy result is to reduce the share of people who want children but postpone them because of housing, childcare, or work conflicts, and to make some countries higher than comparable countries without complementary policy—not to return the OECD as a whole to replacement fertility. The effective unit of fertility policy is a predictable combination of housing, childcare, income, and work arrangements, not a one-off bonus.
- **Audience scale**: The affected population is couples of reproductive age, children, and future public-finance contributors in OECD countries, a hundred-millions-scale constructed impact reach (J-111’s population universe is the 38 OECD members in EXT-119’s 2024-edition Family Database table, deduplicated by member-country ID, not an action denominator); actual repeated actors/behaviour denominator = not applicable. Direct actors are households and national/local institutions; this is not a weekly deduplicated social action.
- **Diffusion-gate review**: Not applicable; this is a demographic-outcome and institutional-combination judgment.
- **Lens**: Historical patterns, population structure, supply and demand, human needs, and social institutions. Technology is only a possible administrative aid.
- **Reasoning chain**: Longer education and employment paths delay partnership and parenthood → the opportunity and housing costs of children are concentrated into a shorter life stage → a cash-only policy covers only part of the price and cannot replace childcare, partnership, or predictable work → France and Nordic combinations show that the gap can be partly compressed; Singapore, Japan, and South Korea show that a single incentive does not automatically restore replacement fertility → the more likely future is modest timing recovery and wider country differences, not a broad OECD rebound.
- **Time window**: 2026-01-01 to 2035-12-31.
- **Falsifier**: Pre-register the denominator as the 38 OECD members in the 2024 edition. By 2035, if at least 26 members have total fertility rates at or above 2.1 for three consecutive years, and both an age-standardised series and a native-born/immigrant composition split still rise (rather than the rise being explained only by age structure, migrant composition, or measurement change), the direction that most remain below replacement is refuted. A few countries reaching 2.1 would not refute the majority claim but would require narrowing the institutional-combination boundary; countries without comparable component data do not enter the numerator, and missingness is not counted as success.
- **Leading indicators**: OECD total fertility rates, age at first birth, and childlessness; childcare enrolment and household out-of-pocket cost; housing burden; female employment and income loss after parental leave; annually.
- **Confidence**: Medium
- **depends-on**: J-109, J-110
- **Strongest opposing mechanism**: Housing supply, childcare, and work institutions could improve together, or a new low-cost care arrangement could let many postponed births occur; alternatively, low-fertility countries could open migration and change the statistical denominator, creating a surface rebound.
- **Against consensus**: Agreement: OECD fertility is generally below replacement and policy bundles matter more than slogans. Divergence: “policy is ineffective” and “policy restores replacement” are not the only choices; the judgment predicts limited recovery and wider country differences. Retained because 2.1, three consecutive years, and composition controls provide an explicit expiry test.

## 5. Put the three clocks back into the social landscape

Housing determines whether a household can begin in a place; migration determines whether labour and care can be added across places; fertility determines the age structure decades later. They interact, but do not substitute for one another: more housing does not create childcare, more migration does not create integration, and a fertility bonus does not create predictable shared life.

**What can be done now:** city planning should track housing permits, population inflow, commuting, and childcare capacity together; care and construction employers should treat migrants' credential recognition, language training, and job quality as supply variables; family-policy evaluation should put first-birth age, childlessness, housing burden, and parental employment continuity in one table. The key warning is not that one demographic forecast may be wrong, but that improving one clock does not make the other two move automatically.

**Still uncovered:** internal migration and city-to-city divergence, religion and marriage norms, wealth distribution's effect on housing access, refugees and irregular migration, and the effects of specific urban housing projects. This chain does not fill those gaps; they should become later chains or evidence tasks rather than turning three cards into a total explanation of population change.
