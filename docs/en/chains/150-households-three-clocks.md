# C15 · Three Clocks of the Household: How Housing, Migration, and Fertility Rework Society

> **This chain is not a one-cause story in which low fertility mechanically produces one future.** It places three observable but interacting constraints together: housing determines whether a household can form in a desired place; migration changes the geography of labour and care supply; fertility turns today's choices into an age structure twenty or thirty years later. AI is not a premise of this chain. It may change information, design, and execution costs, but it cannot replace the hard constraints of housing, borders, care, and time.

## 1. Return to history first: the same policy produces different results under different household constraints

Housing, family policy, and population movement cannot be explained by a rule that says “subsidies make people have more children.” Historical material gives at least two success/failure contrasts:

- **Relatively successful combinations**: France has combined cash, childcare, leave, and tax measures over time; Nordic countries institutionalised compatibility between childcare and mothers' employment. They did not restore post-war fertility, but their long-run fertility rates have generally been higher than in low-fertility East Asian countries. This shows that institutions can move part of the family cost away from households; the cost is not necessarily fixed.
- **Failed single-point interventions**: Singapore has repeatedly introduced pronatalist policies yet has remained low-fertility; Japan expanded childcare without automatically returning to replacement fertility. When housing prices, gendered labour-market arrangements, long hours, and delayed partnership do not loosen together, a single payment or slogan rarely rewrites family formation.
- **Pre-registered four-case comparison**: To avoid selecting only successes, this chain pairs France with Japan and Sweden with Singapore as two contrasts in direction. The success-side rule is a total fertility rate above the OECD bottom quartile in most years from 2010 to 2022, alongside a combined childcare/leave/employment-compatibility policy package. The failure-side rule is a total fertility rate below 1.5 for five consecutive years in the same window despite an explicit pronatalist or childcare-expansion policy. This is a screening rule, not causal proof; the country comparison is checked against the OECD series in EXT-119, and housing and migration mechanisms cannot be extrapolated from these four countries alone.
- **Migration has two sides**: OECD migration material shows that migration can add working-age people and ease some shortages in the short run, but receiving places must also provide housing, schools, status, and political integration. Migration is not a machine that restores the age structure; it moves pressure toward carrying capacity and integration.
- **Historical rule**: policies that durably change family formation usually change who carries time costs, who carries housing costs, and who can obtain predictable status after moving. One-off price changes more often change timing than total births.

This chain therefore does not make “abundance → scarcity” the master explanation. It asks it as an optional question: when housing, care, or migrant supply becomes more abundant, does scarcity move to place, status, time, and predictable relationship commitments?

## 2. J-109: Housing costs will keep delaying household formation into later life and fewer affordable places

Housing is not only an asset price. It is a common entry point to employment, education, care, and partner choice. The OECD housing database provides comparable measures of price-to-income ratios, rents, and housing burden. That supports housing stress as one constraint on family formation, but cannot by itself prove that house prices cause every cross-country birth-rate difference.

## 5. Put the three clocks back into the social landscape

Housing determines whether a household can begin in a place; migration determines whether labour and care can be added across places; fertility determines the age structure decades later. They interact, but do not substitute for one another: more housing does not create childcare, more migration does not create integration, and a fertility bonus does not create predictable shared life.

**What can be done now:** city planning should track housing permits, population inflow, commuting, and childcare capacity together; care and construction employers should treat migrants' credential recognition, language training, and job quality as supply variables; family-policy evaluation should put first-birth age, childlessness, housing burden, and parental employment continuity in one table. The key warning is not that one demographic forecast may be wrong, but that improving one clock does not make the other two move automatically.

**Still uncovered:** internal migration and city-to-city divergence, religion and marriage norms, wealth distribution's effect on housing access, refugees and irregular migration, and the effects of specific urban housing projects. This chain does not fill those gaps; they should become later chains or evidence tasks rather than turning three cards into a total explanation of population change.
