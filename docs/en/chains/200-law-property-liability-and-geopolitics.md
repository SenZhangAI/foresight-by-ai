# C20 · Boundaries Before Capability: How Law, Property, Liability, and Geopolitical Institutions Decide Who Can Act

[中文](../../zh/chains/200-law-property-liability-and-geopolitics.md)

**Lenses not used**: none—this chain uses L1 supply and demand, L4 historical regularities, L5 social regularities, L7 institutions and power, and L8 human nature. Technology capability and “abundance → scarcity” are forces that change the object of exchange and bargaining chips, not the sole engine.

> **Entry point**: When the object of exchange expands from land, machines, and services to data, model outputs, cross-border agents, and critical infrastructure, the first change may not be capability but “who may use, who may license, who answers for an incident, and which institution can adjudicate.” This chain carries the boundaries of law, property, liability, and cross-border institutions. It does not duplicate C14’s electoral, security-budget, and national-security-trigger response, or restate C11’s agency-authority judgment.

## 1. Separate four boundaries first

1. **Use rights**: who may access data, models, interfaces, or critical infrastructure, and whether access can be revoked;
2. **Claim on returns**: who may license, price, transfer, or capture residual value from model outputs;
3. **Liability capacity**: who can remediate harm, who bears proof, insurance, and compensation;
4. **Adjudicative power**: when cross-border services, data flows, and infrastructure dependence conflict, which state, court, platform, or standards body can actually enforce a decision.

Compressing these into “who owns the data” or “does AI have legal personality?” mixes distinct legal questions with distinct power carriers.

## 2. Historical look-back: new objects usually grow layered rights, while cross-border conflict does not converge automatically

Historical material supports a bounded candidate mechanism: when a new object of exchange is replicable, externality-producing, or geographically dependent, institutions often split access, returns, liability, and adjudication into layers; a universal ownership slogan is not universal enforcement.

- **Successful mechanism case one (pending review)**: patent systems split disclosure, exclusion, duration, and licensing. This supports “knowledge exchange needs a set of checks and balances,” not that every innovation can be explained by one property form.
- **Successful mechanism case two (pending review)**: aviation and cross-border communications organize service through licensing, interconnection, liability, and state jurisdiction. This supports “infrastructure first acquires an executable bundle of boundaries,” not that global rules naturally homogenize.
- **Failure comparison one (pending review)**: privacy, content, and data-transfer rules for cross-border internet services remain differentiated, refuting “replicability over networks automatically produces uniform property and liability.”
- **Failure comparison two (pending review)**: software licences and open-source agreements can grant defined use rights but cannot by themselves close training-data provenance, model-output infringement, or cross-border compensation, refuting “one licence text closes all liability.”

These four are calibration candidates awaiting source registration; they do not pretend that the 2+2 historical validation is complete. If definitions, case sources, or counterfactuals cannot be made comparable, the judgments remain open or are downgraded to landscape-only.

## 3. Chain logic: expanding capability first creates boundary disputes, and institutions answer with layered permission and liability carriers

Data and model outputs are replicable, but provenance, privacy, training contribution, and real-world consequences are not fully replicable → parties split access, use, returns, and revocation into different rights → consequential settings remain attached to existing organizations, insurance, and human review rather than automatically generating independent legal personality as models improve → cross-system agent incidents expose proof, recovery, and jurisdiction conflicts → states treat compute, data, chips, cloud services, and critical infrastructure as bargaining assets, rewriting availability through licensing, export controls, procurement, standards, and localization requirements → society sees several institutional zones with different degrees of interoperability, not one globally uniform rulebook.

The boundary of this chain is important: it does not predict which country or platform must win, and it does not expand C14’s electoral and security-budget judgment into war, nuclear deterrence, or global-polarisation forecasts. It only judges that the object of exchange and cross-border dependence put law, property, liability, and adjudicative power on the same institutional ledger.

## 4. Readable future scenes

- **Near term (2027–2030)**: consequential industries first write model use into contracts, audits, insurance, and revocation procedures. Data and model-output disputes show up as licence scope, training provenance, audit trails, and compensation, not as an abstract question of whether “AI owns property.”
- **Middle term (2030–2035)**: cross-border services sort into institutional zones. Firms compare not only model performance but whether data may cross borders, who remedies an incident, whether supply is resilient, and who adjudicates disputes; compliance interfaces and provable liability capacity may become infrastructure.
- **Farther term**: control of critical infrastructure and high-value data may become a durable bargaining asset for states and large organizations. Institutional zones may retain mutual-recognition agreements without naturally converging into one world property law. This is a landscape, not a forecast of war or of one country’s policy.

## 5. What can be done now

- **Researchers**: build separate event panels for data access, model outputs, cross-border agent incidents, and critical-infrastructure dependence. Do not treat the number of legal texts as evidence of enforcement; first fix common denominators for licences, revocations, incidents, compensation, and cross-border execution.
- **Companies and public agencies**: before deployment, write use rights, claims on returns, liability, and adjudication in separate contract and logging fields; mark data export, supply interruption, and dispute-resolution fallbacks for cross-border services.
- **Readers and reviewers**: when told that “AI will automatically become a property subject” or “global rules will quickly converge,” ask whether the claim concerns access, returns, liability, or adjudication, and who can enforce it in reality.

## 6. Judgment boundary

This chain adds J-120 as a formal judgment card and retains J-121 as a landscape-only entrance. J-120 covers layered rights and liability boundaries for data, model outputs, and cross-system agent incidents. J-121 sketches institutional differentiation as critical infrastructure, data, and compute become cross-border bargaining assets, but equivalent common-SLA semantics and independent registration of final failure or abandonment remain open, so it must not be used as an actionable judgment or bet. Both cards preserve the evidence boundary that external comparison is incomplete and that the denominator of actual repeated actors is not yet known; they supplement rather than replace the existing C11, C13, and C14 judgments.

**Boundaries still not carried**: this round does not turn war, nuclear deterrence, energy geopolitics, transnational polarization, a globally unified property law, or independent AI personhood into judgments; C14’s existing political and security entry is not duplicated here. Comparable cross-jurisdiction rule time series, an actual incident population, and common SLAs plus final failure / abandonment records for cross-border services remain explicit gaps for the next round, not evidence that an entry alone can substitute for.
