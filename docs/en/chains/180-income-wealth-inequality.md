# C18 · Income, Wealth, and Inequality: how productivity gains pass through claims into households

[中文版本](../../zh/chains/180-income-wealth-inequality.md)

**Lenses not used**: none—the chain uses L1, L2, L3, L4, and L5; “abundance → scarcity” is a supporting question, not an additional lens.

> **Lens declaration**: C18 starts from the historical relationship among sources of income, asset ownership, inheritance, tax systems, and public services. AI and automation are one force changing production and bargaining, not the sole engine. “Abundance → scarcity” is an optional question here: more output does not by itself equal more equal access to claims, housing, care, or political influence.

## 1. Why this dimension needs its own entrance

A society can have more goods, higher average productivity, and tighter household budgets at the same time. Income determines what can be bought now; wealth determines whether a household can absorb shocks, obtain housing and education, wait for returns, and influence rules over time. Neither can be replaced by average GDP. A capability-first forecast easily turns “can produce” into “can obtain.”

C18 asks: **as productivity, asset prices, and labour demand change, which mechanisms turn gains into broad income, and which leave them with asset owners, scarce positions, and established inheritance chains?**

## 2. Historical calibration: first test whether the rule can say no

### 2.1 Cases supporting the mechanism

- **The mid-twentieth-century high-income economies**: where productivity growth coincided with collective bargaining, progressive taxation, public education, housing construction, and social insurance, wages, public services, and middle-household assets could expand together for a sustained period. This does not prove that growth is automatically equalizing; it shows that institutions can translate productivity into broader command over resources.
- **Post-war East Asian land and education transitions**: where land reform, mass education, and manufacturing expansion reinforced one another, broader groups of households gained entry to new industries. Growth spread through asset starting points and educational access, not through technology alone.

### 2.2 Counterexamples and failed cases

- **Early nineteenth-century industrialization**: greater factory output did not automatically deliver proportionate worker income, housing, or safety. Labour organization, political inclusion, and social insurance arrived later, showing that “more output” cannot by itself imply “better distribution.”
- **Resource-rent and high-asset-price economies**: resource or financial returns can rise while average income grows, yet concentrated land, housing, equity, and inheritance lock new gains into existing ownership. Without institutions that turn rents into public services or broad ownership, growth does not automatically diffuse.

This 2+2 set is currently a set of historical candidates to register, not completed external calibration: it suggests that the rule must permit both “output rises while distribution does not improve” and “institutional intermediaries diffuse gains,” but it supplies no comparable countries, years, measures, or sources. This round therefore cannot claim that historical evidence has validated the rule; J-118 is a working hypothesis with an explicit falsification exit. The next round must calibrate it against concrete historical series and a contemporary cross-country panel rather than treating four macro categories as evidence.

## 3. The chain: from production to claims

1. Technology, capital, and organization increase output from some tasks. Their first effect is on unit cost and task demand, not on ownership.
2. When complementary assets—housing, land, compute, finance, networks, or permits—are more concentrated, rents on scarce positions rise; gains first flow to owners and institutions that carry liability.
3. Whether workers obtain gains depends on substitutability, collective bargaining, occupational entry, geographic mobility, and transfers. The same technology can produce different distributions under different institutions.
4. Wealth gaps feed back through housing, education, care, and the risk of waiting, shaping the next generation’s skills, migration, and political choices.
5. Taxation, public services, housing supply, labour institutions, and broad asset ownership can interrupt that feedback. Without these intermediaries, higher average productivity is more likely first to appear in asset returns, profits, or returns to complementary skills.

## 4. Independent judgment and boundary

### Independent judgment: productivity growth first differentiates claims, then determines whether income diffuses

From 2027 to 2035, in economies affected by high-productivity technologies, increased productivity or output will not automatically become sustainable income and wealth growth for most households. Where housing, capital, and complementary-asset supply is constrained, gains are more likely first to appear in asset returns, rents on scarce positions, and returns to complementary skills; bottom-household disposable capacity catches up only where bargaining power, public services, taxation, or broad asset ownership expand alongside productivity.

## 5. What readers can do next

- **Researchers**: split “growth” into productivity, labour-income share, bottom real disposable income, household net wealth, housing cost, and access to public services, with regional, age, and asset-ownership groups rather than relying on per-capita output.
- **Organizations and policy designers**: before deploying a productivity-increasing technology, list the owners of complementary assets, who carries liability, who has bargaining power, and which housing, training, tax, and public-service conditions are needed for gains to diffuse.
- **General readers**: separate “cheaper / more abundant” from “can I obtain it?” The latter requires watching wages, rents, debt, asset ownership, and institutional transfers together.

This is a distributional social judgment, not a single business-opportunity recommendation; any opportunity still needs a payer and a durable hard constraint.
