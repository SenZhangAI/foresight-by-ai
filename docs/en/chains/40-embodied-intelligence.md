# C4 · Embodied Intelligence: For AI to Pass the Diffusion Gates, What Is Missing Is a Body That Can Bear Consequences

> **This chain extends [C1](10-generation-becomes-free.md) and the five gates in the [retrospect](../01-retrospect.md).** C1 argues that once generation becomes cheap, value concentrates in inputs that cannot be recombined; the retrospect argues that a capability's audience ceiling is set by the headcount and frequency of the activity it serves, not by the ceiling of the technology. Put the two sentences together and an uncomfortable question follows: **if AI can only move information, how many people perform the activity it serves?**
> **In one sentence**: embodied intelligence is the **necessary complement** for AI to pass the diffusion gates, not a **sufficient condition** — without a body the population base is too small; with a body, three separate gates remain (cost per task, priced liability, and scene fragmentation), and none of them opens automatically as model capability improves.

> **Boundary note**: J-073–J-078 were written from the start at the occupational/organizational level. They describe the audience boundary of particular activities, institutions, or scenes; they are not society-wide diffusion claims. C4 is therefore not the only chain that passes the diffusion gate, and these cards must not be rewritten as claims about “all society,” “generally,” or “the norm.”
>
> **A note on identifiers**: this file calls itself C4, but chain identifiers are allocated only in the [chain registry in the README](../../../README.en.md#chain-registry). Until a C4 row appears there, this is a **candidate chain**. It also allocates no judgment identifiers (`J-NNN`): the prose carries reasoning, time windows, indicators, and counterarguments, while the cards are registered by whoever maintains the ledger.

## 1. 2031: a transfer robot sent back

A care home trials a bed-to-chair transfer robot and decides on day eleven to send it back.

It did not fail on capability. The machine recognized the bed edge, planned the lifting posture, and re-levelled when the load shifted; all twelve transfers in the first week completed, none of them pinching anyone. What the nursing supervisor wrote down was a different set of numbers: two aides transfer a resident in about ninety seconds, the machine takes about six minutes, and an aide still has to stand there the whole time — not out of distrust, but because residents talk. Someone says "wait a moment" halfway through, and the machine needs a human to decide whether that means pain, fear, or just wanting to shift position.

What actually sent it back were two small things that began in week three. One resident refused it from the third day, saying being lifted by a machine felt like being cargo. Another resident's family took a photo during a visit and asked the home whether this was about saving on staff. After every use the contact surfaces had to be cleaned and the unit returned to its charger, and on night shift the corridor width it occupied forced another bed's transfer to take the long way round.

What the supervisor finally wrote on the evaluation form is closer to this chain's subject than any performance metric: **"It replaced the lifting, but not the being-there. And here, lifting is one third of a transfer."**

Capability did not stop this machine. It was stopped by **recurring net burden relative to the incumbent human workflow**: the repeated cost of preparation, supervision, cleaning, explanation, and reassurance exceeded the strength and time it saved. The retrospect calls this Gate 5. It cannot merely ask whether each use adds an action; it must subtract saved waiting, price, time, and process cost from added bodily, social, learning, and monetary burden. Only a positive and material net burden accumulates linearly with frequency.

## 2. First, the population arithmetic: if AI can only move information

Gate 1's test is a single question: **how many people perform the activity this capability serves, and how often?** Apply it to an AI that can only move information and the answer narrows immediately.

People whose primary work surface is a screen and whose primary output is information are a **minority** of global employment. Not a negligible minority — it contains the best-paid occupations and is where AI revenue comes from today — but a minority nonetheless. The population sits elsewhere, and those places share one property: **someone has to move mass in the physical world, stay on site, or touch another person's body with their hands.**

A few checkable orders of magnitude are enough to show where the denominator is, without inventing a precise global figure:

- Agricultural employment worldwide still runs to **hundreds of millions of people**, with a markedly higher share in low- and middle-income countries ([FAO employment indicators 2000–2023](https://www.fao.org/statistics/highlights-archive/highlights-detail/employment-indicators-2000-2023-%28july-2025-update%29/en)).
- Domestic workers are counted by the ILO as a group in the **tens of millions**, a large part of it in informal employment ([ILO · Domestic workers](https://www.ilo.org/topics-and-sectors/domestic-workers)).
- In a single country's occupational statistics, "laborers and freight, stock, and material movers, hand" ([O\*NET 53-7062](https://www.onetonline.org/link/localtrends/53-7062.00)) and "home health aides" ([O\*NET 31-1121](https://www.onetonline.org/link/localtrends/31-1121.00)) are both occupations in the **millions**, the latter listed among the fastest-growing. US figures cannot be extrapolated globally; they are used here only as existence evidence that these activities really do occur at million scale and have an official, checkable count.

So the first judgment is hard: **as long as AI can only move information, Gate 1 holds its audience ceiling at the portion of people whose work surface is a screen.** Inside that ceiling it can reach near-total penetration, the way professional editing software did among editors — an excellent business, but on the retrospect's reading, **never the way a whole society does things**. [J-073](../90-ledger.md#j-073--embodied-intelligence-is-the-necessary-complement-for-ai-to-reach-the-physical-labour-population-not-a-sufficient-condition-for-diffusion) records this denominator-shift judgment. There is exactly one way to change that denominator: extend what AI can move from bits to mass.

The second sentence has to follow immediately, or this chain degrades into "the robots are coming": **changing the denominator only releases Gate 1. None of the other four gates opens by itself.**

- **Gate 2 (replacement)**: a machine has to replace an activity someone already performs, not add one on top. The transfer robot replaced the lifting and not the being-there — and being-there is what the buyer was buying.
- **Gate 3 (carrier)**: the carrier for embodied capability is physical space itself. Screen AI's carrier was already built for other reasons (phones, broadband, browsers). A robot's carrier — corridor widths, floor flatness, connector standards, workcell retrofits, row spacing in a field — **was not built for it by anyone, for any other reason.**
- **Gate 4 (decision)**: a plant manager can decide alone to put a cobot on a line. Putting a robot in a ward or a home requires nurses, family, insurers, and regulators to agree at the same time.
- **Gate 5 (recurring net burden)**: preparation, supervision, cleaning, fault handling, and social awkwardness on every use matter only when they remain positive and material relative to the incumbent human workflow.

This chain therefore uses L2 (constraint migration), L4 (diffusion lag), L6 (irreversibility), and L8 (human needs), and treats the five gates as a sieve rather than as rhetoric. It does **not** forecast robot shipments, does **not** forecast changes in total employment, and gives **no** precise global installed-base figure — no checkable global series was obtained this round, and all of it is recorded as gaps in section 12. It reasons about one thing only: **in what order embodied capability reaches which scenes, what the bottleneck is in each, and where "the numbers work locally" gets misread as "this is about to diffuse."**

## 3. Skeleton of the chain

```text
Model capability rises, unit inference cost falls (C1 / technology chain)
        │
        ▼
Information only → audience ceiling = people whose work surface is a screen (Gate 1)
        │
        ▼
To change the denominator → must move mass, stay on site, touch a body
        │
        ├──▶ Perception and policy: fall with model capability (fast variable)
        │
        └──▶ Actuators / deployment / liability: do not (slow variables)
                 │
                 ▼
        Binding constraint moves to "cost per task + priced liability + deployment hours"
                 │
                 ├──▶ Structured + high frequency + semi-cooperative object: arrives first
                 ├──▶ Unstructured but modifiable environment: arrives mid-sequence
                 ├──▶ Touching a body / one-off sites: arrives late, institutions before homes
                 └──▶ Open-ended household tasks: no society-level diffusion inside the window
```

## 4. Care and ageing: the place that needs it most is the hardest to enter

Demographics make care the most certain demand of the five: the number of people needing care is rising, the number willing to do the work is falling, and the gap cannot be closed by processing information — **nobody lifts an elderly person from a bed into a wheelchair through a screen.**

**The bottleneck is not "can it lift," it is "the person is alive throughout."** A human body is not a rigid body: the same transfer needs a completely different torque trajectory for different weights, muscle tone, and pain tolerance, and the person being moved shifts posture, resists, or suddenly goes slack. That demands millimetre-scale position accuracy and newton-scale force control in one loop, bounded by a human safety margin — harder than picking up a cardboard box not by an order of magnitude but by a category. Riken's ROBEAR transfer-robot prototype, released in 2015, demonstrated this clearly enough ([Riken · ROBEAR](https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2015/20150223_2/)): feasibility on the capability side was shown a decade ago, and a decade later it is not standard equipment on any ward. **The decade of silence is itself the evidence** — it shows what is blocking this square is not capability.

**Conservative time window**: through 2030, what scales in institutional care remains **non-contact assistance** — fetching, monitoring, reminders, cleaning, and non-autonomous transfer aids. **Contact transfer as standard institutional equipment: not before 2032–2038**, and first in the handful of countries with the highest labour costs and the heaviest workers'-compensation exposure for nursing injuries. **Home settings do not reach society-level diffusion inside this window (to 2040).** [J-074](../90-ledger.md#j-074--contact-transfer-in-care-crosses-inside-institutions-first-and-homes-reach-no-society-level-diffusion-inside-this-window) records this institutional-before-home judgment as a traceable card.

**Leading indicators (they move before the outcome)**: (1) whether regulators open a distinct approval pathway for contact transfer robots and whether a reimbursement code exists — without reimbursement, institutions buy from their own budget and the adoption curve stays flat; (2) whether staffing standards allow equipment to be counted against required headcount — that is the line that decides ROI, not the purchase price; (3) whether back-injury compensation rates fall in institutions with dense adoption — the hardest indicator to fake, because insurers rather than vendors produce it; (4) whether a production successor to a ROBEAR-class prototype ever appears.

**Hard constraints**: **bodily presence** (the need itself requires someone to be physically there and to touch) compounded by **law / liability** (a party capable of being sued must carry a transfer accident). Neither can be automated away by the same force that produced the abundance: automating rostering, medication checks, and fall-risk scoring completely does not remove one transfer that must physically happen.

**Beating labour cost locally ≠ society-level diffusion**: it is entirely plausible that a large institution in a high-wage country drives the per-transfer cost of a machine below the wage cost of two aides. At that moment the numbers work — and Gates 3 and 5 still stand in the way: preparation, cleaning, supervision, and explaining do not amortize, and a home additionally needs door widths, floors, and bathrooms rebuilt. **In this square, beating labour cost only means procurement departments start doing the arithmetic; it does not mean the practice reaches most of the people receiving care.** The machine in section 1 was sent back without price appearing anywhere in the reason.

## 5. Warehousing and logistics: navigation is solved, hands are not

Warehousing is where embodied intelligence pencils out first, for reasons that are unglamorous but real: goods feel no pain, the building belongs to one legal entity, and the floor is flat.

**The bottleneck has moved from moving to grasping.** Autonomous mobility inside a structured warehouse is largely a solved problem — flat floors, mappable paths, stop-on-anomaly. What is stuck is **unstructured picking**: mixed SKUs, transparent and reflective packaging, soft polybags, occluded stacks, the last item at the bottom of a tote. The difficulty is not average success rate but **the cost of the failure tail**: a missed grasp costs seconds, a mis-pick costs a rework of the whole fulfilment chain plus reputation. So the engineering target in this square is not "can it pick," it is picks per hour, error rate, and who takes over after a failure.

**Conservative time window**: structured movement, pallet handling, and goods-to-person are already diffusing and continue through 2026–2028; **scaled unstructured single-item picking in controlled large warehouses sits conservatively at 2028–2033**; **door-to-door last-mile delivery (stairs, entry systems, rain, dogs, unattended parcels) does not hold inside this window** — it trips Gate 3 (the carrier is someone else's stairwell) and Gate 4 (building management, residents, and platforms must all change) at once. [J-075](../90-ledger.md#j-075--warehousings-bottleneck-has-moved-from-moving-to-grasping-unstructured-picking-arrives-first-and-door-to-door-delivery-does-not-hold-inside-this-window) records this arrival order from movement to grasping and from warehouses to doorsteps.

**Leading indicators**: (1) whether the commercial model shifts from selling machines to **per-pick pricing** with error-rate damages — per-pick pricing means the vendor is willing to price its own failure tail, the most honest signal of maturity; (2) whether third-party logistics insurance quotes a separate rate for robotic picking; (3) whether **deployment hours per site** fall as the installed base grows (see counter three in section 9); (4) whether hiring volumes and wages in occupations like [O\*NET 53-7062](https://www.onetonline.org/link/localtrends/53-7062.00) diverge in adoption-dense regions ahead of the national series.

**Hard constraints**: **physical** (mass must move, time and energy must be spent) compounded by **ownership / privacy** (layout, SKU master data, and order flow are private assets, and any retrofit needs the owner's consent). The second is routinely underrated: it means every deployment is a negotiation with a specific owner, and negotiation cost does not fall with model capability.

**Beating labour cost locally ≠ society-level diffusion**: a single high-throughput, single-shift, tidy-SKU warehouse already pencils out. But the tail of global warehousing is small and mid-sized sites — low throughput, mixed goods, seasonal swings, and a day of downtime hitting every order. **There, capex does not amortize and downtime risk cannot be spread — and that is exactly where most material-handling jobs are.** Treating the large-warehouse case as the sector's case inverts Gate 1: it counts how many warehouses a machine can enter, not how many people's activity it can replace.

## 6. Manufacturing and assembly: the one scaling that already happened also marks the boundary

Manufacturing is the only scene where "robots already scaled" can be cited directly. The International Federation of Robotics reports that factory demand for robots doubled over ten years ([IFR](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/global-robot-demand-in-factories-doubles-over-10-years)). That fact must be used in both directions: it proves embodied capability really can diffuse, and it **draws the boundary conditions under which the diffusion happened**.

**The technical bottleneck is flexible assembly, not repetitive handling.** First, where it diffused: welding, painting, machine tending, palletizing — repetitive motions, rigid workpieces, calibratable positions, fixed takt. Then, where it did not: flexible assembly. A wire harness has to be routed as it deforms; a connector has to know how far to back off and retry when it will not seat; every soft gasket has a different tolerance. Humans do this on immediate tactile feedback; machines need force control plus tolerance compensation, and today that stack costs disproportionately in both hardware and commissioning hours.

There is a subtler bottleneck: **changeover**. A high-volume single-product line amortizes one teaching pass; a high-mix low-volume shop does not, because every product change means reprogramming, recalibrating, and re-validating safety. That is why robot density correlates far more strongly with **batch size** than with wage levels.

**Conservative time window**: handling and welding of rigid parts are mature and continue to diffuse; **flexible assembly (harnesses, connectors, soft materials) becoming routine on high-volume lines sits conservatively at 2028–2034**; **high-mix low-volume small and mid-sized manufacturers move more slowly**, and inside this window hold only locally, in the few industrial clusters with dense systems-integrator ecosystems — not society-level diffusion. [J-076](../90-ledger.md#j-076--manufacturings-one-real-scaling-passed-through-gate-4s-local-closed-loop-flexible-assembly-and-high-mix-low-volume-are-still-outside-the-gate) records manufacturing's local closed loop and the flexible-assembly boundary.

**Leading indicators**: (1) whether the **composition** of new installations in series like IFR's tilts toward assembly and precision manipulation — reading totals alone mistakes palletizing growth for an assembly breakthrough; (2) whether line **changeover time** falls systematically; (3) the ratio of integrator service revenue to equipment revenue — a ratio that stays high says deployment cost is still carried by on-site labour and the scale effect has not arrived; (4) the residual-value curve for used industrial robots (high residuals signal redeployability, which is the signal that fragmentation is being overcome).

**Hard constraints**: **physical** compounded by **ownership / privacy** (a production line is a specific owner's private asset, the retrofit is theirs to authorize, and the lost output during the retrofit is theirs alone to absorb).

**Beating labour cost locally ≠ society-level diffusion**: manufacturing is the easiest place to make this mistake precisely because it has a real scaling to cite. But look at which gate that scaling passed: **Gate 4's clause (c) — the local closed loop**. A single factory can make it work internally without waiting for society. **A factory can close the loop because it is a walled space with one decision-maker and one standard. Care, agriculture, construction, and homes have no such wall.** Extrapolating the factory's diffusion rate to the other four squares assumes that wall is free.

## 7. Agriculture: robotic milking already crossed, harvesting has not

This square holds one sample that crossed and one that failed, and setting them side by side argues better than any derivation.

**What crossed is milking.** Automatic milking systems are standard equipment on mid-to-large dairy farms in high-wage countries. Why did it work? List the conditions and see how strict they are: the action repeats two or three times a day, the location is fixed, **the animal walks into the machine by itself**, the output is measurable and priceable, and failure is contained (one missed cow). **A cow is a semi-cooperative object, and that condition is close to a free subsidy.**

**What failed is selective harvesting.** Abundant Robotics built an apple-harvesting robot, got it to the point of working in orchards, and still shut the business down ([The Robot Report](https://www.therobotreport.com/abundant-robotics-shuts-down-fruit-harvesting-business/)). This is not a capability story, it is a **utilization** story: a harvester works a few weeks a year, and its capex must amortize over those weeks' operating hours; meanwhile row spacing, tree architecture, and variety differ between orchards, so every new customer means re-adaptation. **Seasonality crushes the denominator, fragmentation inflates the adaptation cost in the numerator — squeezed from both ends, cost per task does not fall.**

**Technical bottlenecks**: perception robustness under natural light and occlusion; end effectors handling damageable produce (a bruised apple is downgraded, a strawberry more so); biological variation — no two fruits on one tree share a position or a ripeness.

**Conservative time window**: structured operations (milking, guided seeding and fertilizing, intra-row mechanical weeding) keep diffusing, with 2026–2030 as the scaling window; **selective harvesting stays at local pilots on single crops inside this window and does not become a mainstream practice before 2030**; globally, most of the potential object of agricultural embodiment is not on capital-intensive farms in high-wage countries at all ([FAO employment indicators](https://www.fao.org/statistics/highlights-archive/highlights-detail/employment-indicators-2000-2023-%28july-2025-update%29/en)), which puts society-level diffusion a generation behind equipment feasibility. [J-077](../90-ledger.md#j-077--in-agriculture-what-crossed-is-milking-not-harvesting-seasonality-and-fragmentation-hold-cost-per-task-above-labour) records the milking-versus-harvesting contrast.

**Leading indicators**: (1) whether harvesting equipment earns **repeat business** under a per-acre or per-yield service model — repeat orders say more than first orders about whether the numbers work; (2) whether **annual effective operating hours** per machine rise (cross-crop, cross-season reuse is the only route that rescues utilization); (3) whether seasonal wages diverge in adoption-dense growing regions ahead of the national series; (4) whether insurers or grower cooperatives offer products covering yield loss from machine operation.

**Hard constraints**: **physical** (the machine must move through a field, bounded by weather and a seasonal window; energy and time are incompressible) compounded by **ownership / privacy** (each plot and orchard is a private, unique physical layout, and compute cannot copy it).

**Beating labour cost locally ≠ society-level diffusion**: robotic milking has already beaten labour cost in some countries, and that is precisely what shows how special the crossing conditions are — high frequency, fixed location, semi-cooperative object. Harvesting satisfies none of them. **So "agricultural robotics is proven" is a dangerous sentence: what is proven is milking, not harvesting, and between them sits the whole set of constraints this chain is about.**

## 8. Construction and domestic work: the one-off site, and somebody else's home

These two go together because they share the hardest property: **the work environment is different every time, and it does not belong to whoever is working in it.**

**Construction.** A site is one-off: geometry, ground conditions, trade sequencing, and weather never repeat; tolerances are in centimetres rather than millimetres; and multiple trades wait on each other in one space. FBR's Hadrian bricklaying machine is the most honest specimen here ([FBR · Hadrian](https://www.fbr.com.au/view/hadrian)) — a direction pushed for something on the order of a decade, still at single-operation and demonstration-project scale. What blocks it is not laying speed: **however fast a machine lays brick, it still waits on the preceding trade, on inspection, on permits, and on weather.** Construction's real structural path is to bypass the site altogether — move the work into a prefabrication plant, that is, convert the one-off site into structured space, at which point it degenerates into the manufacturing problem of section 6.

**Domestic work.** This is the hardest square in the chain, and the difficulty is not even execution but **task definition**. "Tidy up" contains classification, value judgment (which object is rubbish and which is a keepsake), privacy boundaries, and household-specific conventions. On ILO's count domestic workers are a group in the tens of millions ([ILO](https://www.ilo.org/topics-and-sectors/domestic-workers)), so the demand is real and already being paid for — but what is bought was never only the motions; it includes judgment, trustworthiness, and the tacit understanding that saves the employer from specifying anything.

**Technical bottlenecks**: construction — localization and tolerance management in unstructured outdoor environments, coupling between trades, safety of temporary structures; domestic — the open-world object and manipulation tail, intent inference from vague instructions, and irreversible failure (what breaks is not *a* cup, it is *that* cup).

**Conservative time window**: **prefabrication keeps diffusing inside this window** (it moves the problem into structured space); **on-site construction robots remain single-operation equipment through 2040** and do not substitute for the site process as a whole; **open-ended household tasks reach no society-level diffusion inside this window** — home embodiment continues as single-task slices (vacuuming, dishwashing, mowing), each narrow, each able to pass Gate 5, and none of them "housework." [J-078](../90-ledger.md#j-078--construction-and-domestic-work-are-blocked-by-the-one-off-site-and-somebody-elses-home-inside-this-window-they-arrive-only-as-single-operation-equipment-and-single-task-slices) records this construction/domestic-work boundary.

**Leading indicators**: construction — (1) whether general contractors' insurance quotes a separate rate for robotic work; (2) whether schedule contracts start carrying default and stoppage clauses for robotic equipment; (3) whether the prefabrication share of work keeps rising. Domestic — (4) whether a general-purpose home robot falls into the durable-goods price band **and** carries a low enough recurring cost per task (no tidying beforehand, no supervision throughout); (5) whether domestic-worker headcount and wages diverge in high-adoption countries.

**Hard constraints**: construction — **law / permitting** (building permits, inspection, and injury liability must land on a party that can be sued) compounded by **physical**; domestic — **ownership / privacy** (someone's home is private space and entry requires authorization) compounded by **trust / relationship** (who gets a key is the product of repeated interaction over time and cannot be generated in one pass).

**Beating labour cost locally ≠ society-level diffusion**: even if a Hadrian-class machine pushes the unit cost of bricklaying below manual labour on some standardized houses, society-level diffusion requires **the site process, insurance rates, and inspection regime to change together** — Gate 4 multi-party coordination, not equipment performance. Domestic work is the same: between a machine folding laundry in a demo video and a hundred million households letting it into the bedroom daily sit ownership, trust, and the cost paid on every use.

## 9. The strongest counterarguments: three mechanisms, each sufficient to cut the chain

The strongest counter to this chain is not "robots are hard." It is three independent mechanisms, each of which on its own severs the link from "capability arrives" to "bodies diffuse." They are not risk disclaimers; they are concrete propositions that can be observed and that can void this chain.

**Counter one: hardware cost per task does not fall with model capability.** Model capability rides the software curve, where training and inference cost can drop an order of magnitude within a year. Actuators, gearboxes, torque sensors, batteries, and structures ride the industrial manufacturing curve, where annual declines are usually single-digit percentages and bounded by materials and capacity. If capex, maintenance, spares, and downtime are all amortized in and **cost per task stays above human labour**, no amount of model capability produces adoption — the decision looks at cost per unit, not at how clever the model is. **If it holds**: every time window in this chain shifts back, and the arrival sequence degenerates into "only the squares that already pencil out will happen." **What to watch**: the time series of cost per task (per pick, per effective operating hour) and whether it **decouples** from the model-capability curve. If hardware bill-of-materials cost shows no order-of-magnitude decline in three to five years, I should withdraw the mid-sequence windows (sections 5 and 6) first.

**Counter two: liability for failure cannot be priced.** Embodied failure produces bodily injury and property damage, irreversibly (L6). Insurance needs actuarial data, actuarial data needs deployed scale, and deployed scale needs affordable premiums — **a closed loop.** If it never opens, embodied deployment stays inside large enterprises that can self-insure off their own balance sheets and never reaches small firms or households. **If it holds**: section 4 (care) and section 8 (construction, domestic) are void outright, and sections 5 and 6 shrink to "a phenomenon inside large enterprises" — which puts the audience ceiling right back on the side Gate 1 was already blocking. **AI would have changed the denominator, and changed it into another equally small one.** **What to watch**: whether dedicated insurance products with quotable rates appear for embodied work, and whether a first set of claims decisions produces citable liability allocation between vendor, operator, and on-site supervisor.

**Counter three: scene fragmentation kills the scale effect.** Software has zero marginal replication cost; embodiment does not. Every warehouse's aisle spacing, every field's row spacing, and every home's door width differ, and deployment, teaching, calibration, and maintenance are **on-site human labour**. If each unit deployed requires a site engineering pass, the scale effect lands on integrator headcount, and integrator headcount does not expand with model capability — **this turns embodied intelligence into a service industry, and service industries do not diffuse exponentially.** **If it holds**: the arrival sequence still stands, but every window multiplies by a coefficient set by labour supply, and society-level diffusion is deferred indefinitely. **What to watch**: whether **deployment hours per unit** fall as cumulative installations rise (does a learning curve exist); the share of adaptation hours when one model is redeployed to a new site; whether integrator service revenue falls as a share of the total.

If any one of the three holds over time, this chain is left with a handful of **local crossings** rather than a road to society-level diffusion. They are written here rather than in a footnote because they deserve testing more than the chain's positive reasoning does.

## 10. Arrival sequence: structured first, unstructured next, somebody else's home last

Stack the constraints from the five sections and the arrival sequence is essentially dictated by the constraints themselves, not by any vendor's roadmap. The sort keys are three: **is the environment structured, is the object cooperative, is failure reversible**.

**Stage one · structured, high-frequency, semi-cooperative or inanimate object** — robotic milking, pallet movement, goods-to-person, fixed-station welding and painting, the leading edge of intra-row mechanical weeding. **Window: already happening; continued diffusion 2026–2030.** Their shared condition is Gate 4's local closed loop: one dairy farmer, one plant manager, one warehouse owner decides alone. **Indicators**: the sector composition of new installations, the appearance of per-pick and per-acre contracts.

**Stage two · unstructured but the environment can be modified** — unstructured picking in warehouses, flexible assembly within bounded ranges, weeding extended to harder crops. **Window: 2028–2034.** The unlocking condition is not capability but two things holding at once: cost per task can be contracted, and error rates can be covered by damages. **Indicators**: penetration of per-unit pricing, the deployment-hours learning curve, residual values of used equipment.

**Stage three · touching a human body, or one-off sites** — contact transfer in care, and **single-process** work on construction sites. **Window: 2032–2040, and institutions and job sites before homes and self-built housing.** The unlocking conditions are priced liability (insurance rates and reimbursement codes) and carrier modification (care protocols, site processes). **Indicators**: dedicated insurance products and rates, changes in injury compensation rates, robotic work itemized in policy wordings.

**Stage four · open-ended household tasks** — the "tidy up" class. **Window: no society-level diffusion by 2040.** It will not arrive as a general-purpose housekeeping robot; it will keep arriving as single-task slices, each replacing one concrete activity and each driving recurring cost close to zero before it diffuses. **Indicator**: recurring cost per use (does it need tidying beforehand, does it need supervision throughout), not capability demos.

**Four overall indicators run across all four stages**, and each moves earlier than any shipment figure: (1) the cost-per-task time series and whether it decouples from model capability; (2) insurance rates for embodied work and the first claims decisions; (3) the learning curve of deployment hours per unit against cumulative installations; (4) occupational counts and wages (for instance [O\*NET 53-7062](https://www.onetonline.org/link/localtrends/53-7062.00) and [31-1121](https://www.onetonline.org/link/localtrends/31-1121.00)) diverging in adoption-dense regions ahead of the national series — noting that this is one country's observation window and cannot be used as a global indicator.

## 11. So who should change what

- **Institutions and employers (care, warehousing, manufacturing, farms)**: change the evaluation question from "can the machine do it" to "**what recurring cost does each use impose**" — preparation, supervision, cleaning, fault handling, human acceptance. The machine in section 1 met every performance spec and was returned entirely on recurring cost. Purchase contracts should price per unit or per effective operating hour, pushing the cost of the failure tail back onto the supplier.
- **Insurers and regulators**: the loop in counter two — no data without rates, no rates without deployment, no deployment without data — **can only be broken by one side moving half a step first**. The feasible half-step is a pilot rate and reimbursement code for bounded scenes (single operation, supervised, clear liable party), trading bounded deployment for actuarial data. Without it, embodied capability stays inside enterprises large enough to self-insure.
- **Founders**: the entry point is not "a general-purpose robot," it is **turning one class of task into a contractable promise about cost and error rate**. Use this chain's three sort keys to pick scenes: structured environment, cooperative object, reversible failure — scenes that win on all three are crowded, scenes that win on none are traps inside this window. The other underrated entry is the **carrier**: corridors, floors, workcells, row spacing, interface standards. Gate 3 was always satisfied by someone building the carrier for another reason; whoever builds it collects the rent.
- **Workers and unions**: what arrives first is not the replacement of a whole occupation but **the replacement of its most structured segment**. The negotiation is therefore not "robots or not" but "which segment goes, and how is the remaining segment repriced" — the supervisor's sentence in section 1 is the starting point: lifting is one third of a transfer.
- **Policy and training systems**: if this chain holds, the earliest exposure is in **structured material-handling occupations** and the latest is in work that requires touching a body and inferring intent. Directing training resources toward the latter is more defensible than directing them at "learning to work alongside robots."
- **This project itself**: never write "the numbers work in this scene, in this place" as "this is about to diffuse." Every section above carries the same sentence, because it is the easiest error to make and the hardest to notice.

## 12. Evidence boundary: what I know and what I do not

**External material used here** (for comparison and existence proof only; it does not write back into the reasoning): [FAO employment indicators 2000–2023](https://www.fao.org/statistics/highlights-archive/highlights-detail/employment-indicators-2000-2023-%28july-2025-update%29/en), [ILO domestic workers](https://www.ilo.org/topics-and-sectors/domestic-workers), [O\*NET 53-7062](https://www.onetonline.org/link/localtrends/53-7062.00), [O\*NET 31-1121](https://www.onetonline.org/link/localtrends/31-1121.00), [Riken ROBEAR](https://www.riken.jp/en/news_pubs/research_news/pr/2015/20150223_2/), [IFR](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/global-robot-demand-in-factories-doubles-over-10-years), [Abundant Robotics shutdown](https://www.therobotreport.com/abundant-robotics-shuts-down-fruit-harvesting-business/), [FBR Hadrian](https://www.fbr.com.au/view/hadrian).

**Agreement**: external material supports two directions — robot adoption inside factories really has diffused materially on a ten-year scale (IFR), and technical feasibility is not commercial feasibility (Abundant Robotics shut the business after reaching field-capable hardware). The second agrees closely with this chain's independent reasoning and must be labelled honestly: **it is not this chain's discovery. What this chain adds is a structural explanation of that class of failure (utilization × fragmentation) and the observable leading indicators.**

**Divergence and boundaries**: external material mostly discusses **installation totals** or **single cases**, whereas this chain is about **arrival order** and **where the constraint sits**; the former does not imply the latter. O\*NET covers one country only; the definitions, years, and statistical boundaries of the FAO and ILO series are in their own links, and only their **order of magnitude** is used here, never a precise value.

**Not obtained this round, recorded as gaps** (no estimate is dressed up as a figure): (1) global deployment scale and retention of embodied robots in care settings — whether any production successor to a ROBEAR-class prototype exists, no checkable source found this round; (2) cost-per-task time series by scene — this chain's single most important indicator, with no public cross-scene series available; (3) insurance rates and claims decisions for embodied work — not obtained; (4) the learning curve of deployment hours per unit — vendors do not publish it, and it can only be inferred indirectly from integrator service revenue share; (5) any crossing of global installed base against employment — this chain **uses no precise global figure**.

**Why I still hold the line**: the core argument depends on none of the missing numbers. It depends on a structural difference — **perception and policy fall along the software curve, while actuators, deployment, and liability fall along industrial and institutional curves** — and the slope gap between those curves is objective. Even if embodied capability advances far faster than I expect, Gate 3 (carrier), Gate 4 (multi-party decision), and Gate 5 (recurring cost) each still have to be unlocked, and none of them unlocks with model capability. If cost per task is observed falling in step with model capability, and deployment hours falling markedly with installed base, I withdraw counters one and three in section 9 first, and then move the whole arrival sequence in section 10 forward.

> **Current status**: this is a mid-term chain whose tests are cost per task, insurance rates, and deployment hours. It allocates no judgment identifiers; the corresponding cards (the five required fields, confidence, status) are registered by the ledger maintainer, and the chain identifier is whatever the [chain registry in the README](../../../README.en.md#chain-registry) says it is. It is the same structure as [C3](30-power-land-and-permits.md) projected once more: C3 says the constraint on compute lands on grids and permits that cannot be shipped; this chain says the constraint on intelligence lands on bodies, liability, and sites that cannot be copied.
