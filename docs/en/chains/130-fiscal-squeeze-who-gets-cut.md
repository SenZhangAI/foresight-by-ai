# C13 · Fiscal Squeeze: Who Gets Cut First

[中文版](../../zh/chains/130-fiscal-squeeze-who-gets-cut.md)

[Back to README / document map](../../../README.en.md)

London, 17 November 2022. Less than two months earlier, an unfunded tax-cutting budget had pushed the UK government bond market to the edge. The tax cuts were announced on 23 September. On 28 September the Bank of England announced temporary purchases of long-dated government bonds, warning that if the dysfunction continued or worsened “there would be a material risk to UK financial stability”. On 20 October, Prime Minister Liz Truss resigned. On this day the new Chancellor, Jeremy Hunt, stood in the House of Commons and announced an austerity budget.

Yet inside this austerity, one sum was not cut by a single penny. Margaret, a retiree (a fictional character), will get a letter: her State Pension rises with inflation — in Hunt's words, “the biggest ever cash increase in the state pension” — £870 more a year. Her grandson, a nurse, gets no letter at all. His personal allowance stays at £12,570, and so does the higher-rate threshold; Hunt extended the freeze on these thresholds “for a further two years taking us to April 2028”. Wages rise with inflation, the thresholds do not move, and each year a slice of income quietly falls into a higher tax band. Not one line tells him that he now pays more tax.

Two years before that came an even quieter cut. In November 2020 the UK cut foreign aid from 0.7% to 0.5% of gross national income. In February 2025 it announced a further cut to 0.3%, with the savings going to defence. The people who lose clinics and vaccine programmes live in other countries and do not vote in the UK.

> This chain starts from public finance and sovereign debt and does not presuppose any AI capability; NATO's 2025 defence commitment enters only as a signed budget requirement, and this chain does not reason about geopolitics itself. None of the three cards passes Gate 1; all are written as institutional or segment judgments.

## 1. Three bills on one budget

In the 2020s the public finances of rich democracies received three bills at once. None of them needs any new technology:

- **Interest**: the stock of debt plus higher interest rates. A report by the US Government Accountability Office (GAO) states that net interest spending exceeded defence spending in fiscal year 2024, and still did in fiscal 2025. The US Congressional Budget Office (CBO) reports that in fiscal 2025 net interest on the public debt exceeded $1 trillion for the first time (a public-debt interest measure, broader than the net interest GAO compares with defence).
- **Ageing**: more and more people draw public pensions, while the number paying contributions grows more slowly. The 2026 annual report of the US Social Security trustees (released 9 June 2026) projects that the Old-Age and Survivors Insurance (OASI) trust fund will be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032; after that, income covers only 78% of scheduled benefits. Combined with the disability fund, the depletion point is the third quarter of 2034.
- **Defence**: at the Hague summit on 25 June 2025, NATO allies agreed to spend 5% of GDP a year on defence and security-related spending by 2035, of which at least 3.5% is core defence, and to submit “annual plans showing a credible, incremental path”, with a review in 2029. In NATO's 2026 data, core defence spending as a share of GDP in the UK, France, Germany and Italy was 2.22%, 1.88%, 1.60% and 1.46% in 2023, and is estimated at 2.56%, 2.22%, 2.69% and 2.10% in 2026.

The three bills land on the same budget, and taxes cannot rise without limit, so some spending always has to give up share. This chain does not ask “will public finances get into trouble?” It asks: **when share has to be given up, who does the knife fall on first?** The UK gave one answer in autumn 2022: people already drawing were not touched; people not yet drawing, people who cannot see the bill, and people who do not vote in the country gave way first.

## 2. History: where the knife fell, and where it did not

The precedents below are not rules that passed 2+2 calibration; they are narrative comparisons only. The only part that can be used as a rule is L10's veto direction.

**The knife fell on people who had not yet started drawing:**

- **United States, 1983**: the Social Security Amendments (signed 20 April 1983) raised the full retirement age gradually from 65 to 67, starting with people who turned 62 in 2000. At the time these people were still working, 17 years away from drawing. The same law also touched current recipients, but in ways that did not change the nominal amount: the cost-of-living adjustment was delayed by six months, and benefits of higher-income recipients became taxable (affecting about one in ten recipients in 1984).
- **Canada, 2012 and 2016**: the 2012 budget announced that the eligibility age for Old Age Security (OAS) would rise from 65 to 67, taking effect from 2023; the 2016 budget announced its reversal before it took effect. This shows that cutting future cohorts is **within reach, but does not necessarily stick**.
- **United States, 2005**: the Social Security personal-accounts proposal first promised “no change” to people aged 55 and over and touched only younger cohorts; none of the 10 reform bills received congressional action. Touching only future cohorts can still meet the defenders of the whole arrangement — this is one of L10's calibration cases; see [Retrospect section 13](../01-retrospect.md#13-the-first-calibration-of-social-regularities-who-draws-the-benefit-and-who-pays-the-bill-2026-10-10).
- **France, 2023 and 2025**: in 2023 the statutory retirement age was raised from 62 to 64, setting off long protests; the social security financing law of 30 December 2025 suspended this increase until 2028.
- **China, 2024**: on 13 September, the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress decided to raise the statutory retirement age gradually. China is not a democracy, yet it too chose to delay retirement step by step by birth cohort.

**In countries that can finance themselves, new legislation has almost never touched current recipients' nominal amounts:**

- **United Kingdom, 2022**: the triple lock was kept, and the State Pension rose in line with inflation (the speech's own words, an £870 increase a year; the matching September 2022 inflation rate of 10.1% was seen only in search snippets this round).
- **France, 2026**: in its draft 2026 social security financing bill the government proposed freezing all basic pensions; the National Assembly deleted the provision, and basic pensions rose by 0.9% by law on 1 January 2026.
- **Germany**: §68a of Book VI of the Social Code provides that when the formula yields a pension point value below the existing value, the existing value is not reduced; the pension package passed in December 2025 extends the 48% floor on the pension level to 2031.

**The exceptions, and the boundaries they show:**

- **Losing market financing and entering a creditor programme**: between 2010 and 2018 Greece cut current recipients' pensions several times, and some recipients lost up to 40% in total; in 2012 Portugal suspended the holiday and Christmas allowances of people drawing pensions through the public social security system, and Constitutional Court ruling No. 353/2012 declared this unconstitutional but let it stand for 2012 itself. **Ireland, also in a programme, did not cut the weekly rate of the State Pension in its 2011 budget** (this round's source covers only that budget); it cut public servants' pensions and benefits for the working-age population.
- **Automatic rules written before T**: Japan changed its indexation formula by legislation in 2016; from 2021, when wages change by less than prices, people already drawing are also adjusted by wages. As a result, all pensions were cut by 0.4% in nominal terms in fiscal 2022. No one voted to cut pensions that year.
- **Touching only high pensions**: Italy's 2018 budget law cut pensions above €100,000 a year by 15%–40% on the part above that level, and Constitutional Court judgment No. 234/2020 shortened the measure from five years to three.

Put together, the order is: **first cut programmes with no domestic periodic recipients, then cut cohorts who have not yet started drawing, and only last current recipients — and for current recipients, countries that can finance themselves would rather use methods that never appear in a notification letter, such as threshold freezes, taxation and automatic formulas, than pass a new law that cuts nominal amounts.** In this order only the first step is carried by L10 (veto direction: within reach); the other two steps are marked as carried by no lens, as declared at the head of this page.

## 3. J-105: current recipients' nominal pensions are the hardest to cut

> **[J-105 · Under fiscal squeeze, current recipients' nominal pensions are the hardest to cut; the knife falls first on those not yet drawing](../ledger/103-110.md#j-105--under-fiscal-squeeze-current-recipients-nominal-pensions-are-the-hardest-to-cut-the-knife-falls-first-on-those-not-yet-drawing)** (confidence: Medium)

From 2026-10-10 to 2034-12-31, no G7 country that can still borrow on the market and has not entered a creditor assistance programme passes **new legislation** that lowers the nominal amount of the median current recipient's basic public pension.

The hardest test is in the United States. OASI is projected to be depleted in the fourth quarter of 2032, and after that its income covers only 78%. Current law writes in no automatic cut. A report by the Congressional Research Service (CRS) says that the Antideficiency Act does not allow spending beyond available funds, so the Social Security Administration would have no authority to pay full benefits on time, and that it is not clear exactly what action the agency would take. This card judges that the gap will not be resolved in a way that leaves people already drawing with less than the amount due under law; in any fix, at least half of the long-term benefit-side savings come from people who have not yet started drawing at the time of enactment (as scored by the Social Security Administration's Chief Actuary).

What is excluded and what counts are written in advance: automatic indexation rules already in force at T (the Japan 2022 kind) **do not count**; cuts aimed only at high pensions (the Italy 2019 kind) **do not count**; US **inaction** that leads to payments below the amount due, or to late payment, **counts as falsification**.

**Independence test**: delete J-001 and every card premised on AI/compute, and this card still holds. The mechanism uses only the fiscal bills, the structure of recipients and the legislative record.

## 4. J-106: aid gives way to defence

> **[J-106 · Aid gives way to defence: the budget with no domestic periodic recipients is squeezed out first](../ledger/103-110.md#j-106--aid-gives-way-to-defence-the-budget-with-no-domestic-periodic-recipients-is-squeezed-out-first)** (confidence: Medium)

Foreign aid is L10's cleanest sample: no one in the donor country draws it on a recurring basis. From 2023 to 2025, the UK's official development assistance (ODA) as a share of gross national income fell from 0.58% to 0.44%, France's from 0.50% to 0.42%, and Germany's from 0.79% to 0.56%; over the same period, core defence spending as a share of GDP rose in all three. Across the members of the OECD Development Assistance Committee (DAC), the ODA share fell to 0.26% in 2025, 23.1% less than the year before in real terms. Italy is the exception: its share rose slightly in 2025, to about 0.3%.

This card judges that, checked against 2028 data published in 2029, the ODA shares of the UK, France and Germany are all still below their own 2023 levels, and their core defence shares are all still above their own 2023 levels; the UK's ODA share is no higher than 0.35%, that is, the 0.3% announced in 2025 has not been reversed; and Italy's does not exceed 0.35%.

This is a “no swing back” judgment, and it agrees with mainstream reporting — the card says so. Its value is not novelty. It writes the claim as a number that can be checked when due. L10's veto direction says only that the cut was reachable: aid has no protection from domestic beneficiaries. L10 does not answer timing and does not guarantee against a swing back — the step "it will not be pulled back to 2023 within the window" rests on the budget record and is carried by no lens in L1–L10.

**Independence test**: delete J-001 and every card premised on AI/compute, and this card still holds.

## 5. J-107: working on past the old retirement age — counted once

> **[J-107 · Paid weekly work at 65+ stops at the D tier: tens of millions, short of a hundred million](../ledger/103-110.md#j-107--paid-weekly-work-at-65-stops-at-the-d-tier-tens-of-millions-short-of-a-hundred-million)** (confidence: Medium)

The most direct way a fiscal squeeze reaches individuals is a later pension age, so that some people keep going to work every week at an age when they would otherwise have retired. Before collecting any number, this chain first committed a separate [freeze record](../../evidence/older-workers-freeze-2026-10-10.en.md): the action is “aged 65 or over and working at least one hour for pay in the reference week”, the denominator is the de-duplicated head count from each country's labour force survey, and the sum across countries counts only as a lower bound (countries not covered are simply not counted).

Counted under the frozen measure, the components of seven countries sum to about 51.64 million: the United States 11.37 million, Japan 9.43 million, India about 23.15 million (a share multiplied by a base), South Korea 4.15 million, the United Kingdom 1.65 million, Canada 1.13 million and Australia 0.76 million; no qualifying EU figure from 2023 onward was obtained, and Mexico's about 3.5 million was seen only as relayed by the media, so under the rules both count as 0. This sum is not yet the qualifying sum: the freeze requires unpaid family helpers to be subtracted where the survey reports them separately, and the US, Japanese and South Korean surveys all do, but this round obtained no separate 65+ count, so the subtraction was not carried out. So, as far as unpaid family helpers go, the about 51.64 million is only an upper bound; the lower bound derived from Japan and South Korea is about 10.29 million (the South Korea item is a cross-year inference). Both bounds are in the tens of millions, and the verdict is **C · D tier only**. The published employment rate of 54.5% for Japan's 65–69 group includes family workers; whether it stays above half after subtracting them was not verified, and it does not carry the verdict.

This result holds only under the frozen measure. China publishes only age-composition shares, so no qualifying count of employed people aged 65+ can be obtained, and under the rules China counts as 0; but China has about 99 million employed people aged 60+, and as soon as one qualifying 65+ figure appears, the sum could cross 100 million. Country-by-country definitions, the gaps and this warning are in the [verdict and check log](../../evidence/older-workers-verdict-2026-10-10.en.md).

**Independence test**: delete J-001 and every card premised on AI/compute, and this card still holds.

## 6. Where this chain meets the others

These are cross-edges, not dependencies of this chain:

- **[C9 · Ageing and Institutional Care](90-aging-care-and-institutional-substitution.md)**: from the gap in family time, C9 derives a shift of care toward formal services and public payment; this chain shows that this public payment competes with pensions for the same budget. Care spending also has people who draw it on a recurring basis, and here too L10 can say only that its outright repeal would meet defenders.
- **[C7 · Technology Arrives First, Power Later](70-capability-to-social-consequences.md)** and [C1](10-generation-becomes-free.md): if AI really raises the growth rate, the arithmetic of debt and interest rates loosens and the squeeze in this chain eases; if AI's gains first concentrate with a few asset owners ([J-090](../ledger/81-90.md#j-090--productivity-gains-first-concentrate-with-scarce-complementary-asset-owners-competition-and-institutions-decide-whether-they-spread)), the tax base may not widen in step. Either direction changes only the strength of the squeeze, not this chain's order, and this chain takes neither as a premise.
- **[C3 · Electrons on the Ground](30-power-land-and-permits.md)**: C3 writes about whom the bill lands on; this chain writes about whom the loss of budget share lands on. Both ask “who can see the cost?”

## 7. Strongest counterarguments and boundaries

- **The bond market can rewrite the order overnight.** The UK in 2022 showed that public finances that lose credibility can be forced to change course within weeks. If a large country suddenly loses market financing within the window, it lands on the Greek and Portuguese side of the boundary, which J-105 does not cover in the first place. The real risk is that “not covered” itself gets abused: this card writes “entering a creditor programme” as an exclusion, so at the due check it must be recorded truthfully whether any country fell into one, and it cannot be used as an excuse after the fact.
- **Automatic formulas are a back door.** Japan shows that a nominal cut can happen without a new vote. If, within the window, a country writes into **new legislation** a formula that triggers a nominal fall within the window, it counts as falsification under this card's terms.
- **Cuts to future cohorts also get reversed or suspended.** Canada in 2016 withdrew the pension-age increase announced in its 2012 budget, and France in 2025 suspended its 2023 legislated increase until 2028. So this chain says only that “the knife falls first on those not yet drawing”, not that this cut is sure to stick.
- **This chain does not reason about geopolitics.** NATO's 5% target enters only as a signed budget requirement; whether it will be met, and whether the 2029 review will change it, lies outside this chain's judgment.
- **Scale**: none of the three cards passes Gate 1. J-105 and J-106 judge legislative and budget behaviour, which affects more than a hundred million pension recipients and aid recipients, but they are not an individual action that can be counted weekly; J-107 reaches only the D tier under the frozen measure. This page does not use phrasing such as “the whole of society” or “universal”.
