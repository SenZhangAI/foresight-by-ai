# C17 · Religion and Belief: Community Does Not Disappear in Lockstep with Modernisation

[中文版](../../zh/chains/170-religion-and-belief.md)

**Lenses not used**: L2, L3, L6, L7, L9

> **Chain entrance**: This chain starts from religion as a carrier of identity, ritual, mutual aid, and norms—not from AI or “abundance → scarcity.” It does not assume that religion must revive, and it does not treat “secularisation” as one global direction. It separates education, urbanisation, intergenerational transmission, migration, and community supply before asking which functions are taken over by other institutions.

## 1. Define the question before forecasting

“Will religion disappear?” is not one testable question. At least four observables must be separated:

1. **Affiliation**: whether a person reports belonging to a religion or tradition;
2. **Practice**: the frequency of worship, prayer, fasting, rites, and community participation;
3. **Institutional carrier**: whether religious organisations provide education, care, charity, marriage/funeral services, and mutual aid;
4. **Norms and identity**: whether religion still shapes fertility, family roles, clothing, calendars, and political identity.

One society can show lower affiliation, lower practice, and continuing local institutional influence—or stable formal affiliation while practice and intergenerational transmission weaken. Collapsing all four into a single “religious strength” index turns different mechanisms into one story.

## 2. Look back first: modernisation changes carriers, not automatically the endpoint

Literacy, urban life, state education, and scientific explanation can weaken a religious organisation’s monopoly over knowledge and public services; this supports a mechanism of authority reorganisation. It does not by itself imply synchronous global secularisation:

- **Positive-mechanism case**: as state education, urban life, and administrative institutions expanded, some European societies saw churches lose parts of their monopoly over attendance, marriage, and funerals. Modern institutions can therefore take over functions once carried by religious organisations.
- **Reverse-mechanism case**: when migration networks, revival movements, or national identity reconnect belief to family, politics, and mutual aid, affiliation and public mobilisation can persist or recover. Demography and identity competition can offset one-way secularisation.
- **Failure probe one**: if “more education means less belief” held across institutions and generations, high education combined with strong community and religious identity should keep disappearing. If it does not, the rule is only local.
- **Failure probe two**: if “community need automatically sustains religion” held everywhere, societies where welfare, schools, and public organisations strongly substitute for religious carriers should retain the same practice frequency. If practice continues to fall, institutional substitution can weaken some religious functions.

This is not a value judgement about any religion. It limits the transferable mechanism to carriers, intergenerational transmission, and identity competition; historical cases are not treated as global percentage forecasts.

## 3. Chain logic: why differentiation and recombination come before one global endpoint

Education and urbanisation reduce information closure and the monopoly of traditional organisations → formal affiliation, fixed rituals, and intergenerational transmission weaken for some groups → marriage, funerals, mutual aid, moral identity, and migration networks still need organisational carriers → religious organisations, secular charities, state services, and digital communities compete over those functions → migration, demography, state regulation, and family transmission differentiate the result across societies → the most likely early observation is **reorganisation of practice, organisational boundaries, and generations**, not every society moving toward “no belief.”

Online content becoming abundant does not make shared rituals, mutual-aid commitments, and belonging infinitely reproducible. The ability to organise people to carry commitments across time and place remains constrained by trust, governance, finance, law, and physical presence. Secular organisations can also take over those functions, so this does not automatically produce a religious business opportunity.

## 4. A readable future scene

- **Near term (2027–2030)**: cross-place and cross-generation differences will be more informative than a “global average” direction. Low practice in highly educated cities can coexist with strong religious identity and migration networks elsewhere; online preaching and abundant content change access, not necessarily durable membership.
- **Mid term (2030–2035)**: conflicts around fertility, care, migration integration, and public education may reveal religion’s social role more clearly than the binary question of belief. The institutional weight of an organisation will depend on whether it can provide trusted mutual aid, clear exit, and intergenerational transmission.
- **Farther term**: religion may split from a single comprehensive institution into different combinations of ritual, identity, charity, ethical education, and political mobilisation. Some combinations may decline while others strengthen under migration or crisis. This remains a landscape, not a precise forecast of global affiliation counts.

## 5. What can be done now

- **Researchers**: do not collect only one “religious or not” column; build a common panel for affiliation, practice frequency, ritual participation, intergenerational transmission, organisational services, and migration background.
- **Public institutions**: when evaluating marriage, funerals, care, education, or migration-integration policy, compare religious and secular organisations as institutional carriers, recording coverage, trust, exit, and responsibility boundaries.
- **Readers**: when confronted with “modernisation inevitably eliminates religion” or “crisis inevitably revives religion,” ask whether the claim concerns affiliation, practice, institutional function, or identity politics, then check its denominator and time window.

These are research and governance actions, not a business promise; this chain does not commodify religious experience or community relations.

## 6. Boundary of the current evidence

This chain currently has one formal judgment card (J-117). It forecasts that differentiation and functional recombination will precede synchronous global decline; its audience field distinguishes affected population from the repeated-action denominator. It does not claim to know the precise global share of affiliation, and it does not upgrade the chain into “religious return” or “religious disappearance.” Comparable long-run data on practice, generations, and institutional functions remain incomplete; where denominators cannot be compared, the result must be recorded as indeterminate rather than treated as support.
