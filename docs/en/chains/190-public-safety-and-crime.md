# C19 · Public Safety and Crime: Safety Is Not Automatically Produced by One Technology

[中文版](../../zh/chains/190-public-safety-and-crime.md)

**Lenses not used**: none—the chain uses L1 supply and demand, L4 historical regularities, L5 social regularities, L8 human nature, and L9 institutions and power; AI and “abundance → scarcity” are not additional engines.

> **Chain entrance**: Public safety is not one curve called “will the crime rate fall?” Separate violence, property crime, online fraud, domestic and sexual violence, public-space safety, and the legitimacy of enforcement before asking how demography, urban opportunity, institutional capacity, and technical change alter each risk. Perceived safety, victimisation, and state records are not the same denominator.

## 1. Separate the question first

At least five observables must be kept apart:

1. **Actual harm**: incidence and losses from violence, theft, fraud, domestic and sexual violence;
2. **Exposure and reporting**: who is exposed, who reports, and which victims never enter administrative records;
3. **Space and relationship**: whether risk occurs in streets, homes, workplaces, platforms, or cross-border networks;
4. **Governance response**: how police, courts, prisons, community organisations, platforms, and insurers divide work, and whether enforcement is appealable;
5. **Safety and trust**: whether people change travel, transactions, neighbourhood contact, and trust in strangers.

If only reports are counted, stronger enforcement and higher willingness to report can be mistaken for more crime. If only fear is counted, media attention and political mobilisation can be mistaken for more actual harm.

## 2. Look back first: effective decline and displacement can coexist

Historical material supports a limited mechanism: risk is recombined by demographic structure, opportunity, governance capacity, and tools. More police or a new technical system alone does not guarantee lower total harm.

- **Mechanism success case one (to be rechecked)**: violent crime fell over long periods in some US cities in the 1990s alongside changes in age structure, employment and spatial governance, policing, and drug markets. This supports “multiple intermediaries must move together” rather than attributing the decline to one policing technology.
- **Mechanism success case two (to be rechecked)**: Japan’s long post-war level of lethal violence was associated with community norms, demography, weapon availability, police reach, and social control. This supports “low crime requires a bundle of institutional and social conditions,” not a universal extrapolation from one country.
- **Failure counter-case one**: the US “tough drug enforcement—mass incarceration” path did not turn harsher punishment automatically into sustained declines in every crime category, and it imposed new family and community costs. It refutes “more punishment necessarily means more safety.”
- **Failure counter-case two**: identity systems, internet controls, and anti-fraud technology have expanded while cross-border network fraud can still grow or move. It refutes “better detection automatically eliminates online crime.”

These four groups calibrate candidate mechanisms; a common definition, time window, and counterfactual have not yet been registered reproducibly. Their purpose is to reject “one technology determines safety,” not to manufacture certainty about the future.

## 3. Chain logic: visible violence and online loss differentiate, while governance decides whether harm falls

Ageing and urban-space change exposure structures → education, employment, and community support change available opportunities and conflict settings → police, courts, platforms, and insurers change detection probability, reporting cost, and recovery → sensors, identity systems, and generative tools lower the cost of evidence and defence on one side, while lowering the threshold for fraud and forgery on the other → crime is redistributed among streets, homes, institutions, and platforms, while victimisation data and perceived safety diverge → only when cross-institution responsibility, appeals, cross-border recovery, and victim support are available together can technical “detection” become lower actual harm rather than more records or displaced risk.

The hard constraint is that more cameras, models, and data cannot substitute for lawful authority, trusted reporting, cross-institution coordination, and correction of wrongful enforcement. Treating “predicted risk” as “proof of guilt” turns public safety into a new power risk.

## 4. A readable future scene

- **Near term (2027–2030)**: visible street violence may continue to fall or remain low in some high-income cities, while fraud, identity theft, and organised property crime borne by older people, migrants, small businesses, and platform users become more prominent. Reporting rates, platform interception, and actual losses may all move at once; no single number is enough.
- **Mid term (2030–2035)**: policing technology will move from “find more” toward “explain, appeal, correct, and recover across borders.” Institutions that connect platforms, banks, police, courts, and victim support may reduce losses; automated monitoring without responsibility boundaries may increase error and distrust.
- **Farther term**: public safety may split into two ledgers: a physical-space ledger of violence and order, and an information-space ledger of identity, payment, extortion, and manipulation. Both affect mobility and trust in strangers, but they need not be solved by the same institution, technology, or policy. This is a landscape, not a claim that crime must rise or that technology must produce safety.

## 5. What can be done now

- **Researchers**: build a common panel across at least four regions and at least two societies per region, separating victimisation surveys, reports, platform-fraud losses, lethal violence, enforcement contact, and perceived safety; fix definitions, denominators, and reporting bias before comparing trends.
- **Public institutions**: treat online fraud and offline violence as different governance chains, publishing interception, false-positive, recovery, appeal, and victim-support measures; every automated identifier should record error rates, an appeal route, and the responsible party.
- **City and platform managers**: do not use camera counts or model recall as safety outcomes; before deployment specify revocation boundaries, human review, retention limits, and victim remedy.
- **Readers**: when told “more police means safer,” “AI surveillance will eliminate crime,” or “more reports mean more crime,” ask whether the claim concerns actual victimisation, records, losses, fear, or governance capacity.

## 6. Boundary of the current evidence

This chain currently has one formal judgment card (J-119). It does not forecast a one-way global rise or fall in crime; it tests whether risk recombination comes before an overall improvement in safety from 2027 to 2035: some visible violence may fall while online fraud and identity-based property losses expand or move, with the final outcome depending on governance, recovery, and legitimacy. Historical cases supply two success and two failure comparisons for rule formation, but a common cross-country database, victim denominator, and external-source register are not complete; where definitions cannot be aligned, the falsifier must be recorded as indeterminate.
