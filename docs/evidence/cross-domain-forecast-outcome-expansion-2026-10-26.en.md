## 1. Scope and stop rule

This page does one bounded job: it places the **target, judgment time, result-side material, and missing fields** from publicly traceable records in one reviewable register. Each case separates:

- what the forecast or target actually was, rather than what later narrative says it should have predicted;
- `T`, the forecast or information-cutoff time, and whether it can be fixed to a date;
- metric, unit, denominator, and target date;
- whether the outcome window really covers the target;
- whether the original material is locatable—secondary retellings cannot impersonate originals;
- what is missing and whether the gap blocks judgment.

If the forecast object, outcome object, metric, unit, denominator, or observation window cannot be closed, the record remains `UNKNOWN/UNVERIFIED`. A passing structure check, more sources, or an outcome that “looks bad” cannot substitute for those fields.

## 3. Evidence boundaries by case

### 3.1 Y2K: cost estimates, appropriations, actual spending, and disaster outcomes are different metrics

The GAO report *Year 2000 Computing Crisis: Costs and Planned Use of Emergency Funds* (AIMD-99-154, 1999-04-28) provides locatable pre-outcome cost material: the 24 major federal agencies’ total estimate rose from `$2.3bn` in February 1997 to about `$7.5bn` in February 1999. It also lists FY1999 and FY2000 estimates and the structure of 1999 emergency appropriations.

This is not yet a complete “Y2K forecast hit / miss” pair:

1. the report explicitly warns that reported costs were generally estimates rather than actual costs;
2. appropriations, allocations, obligations, actual expenditures, and opportunity costs are not the same denominator;
3. “no global infrastructure collapse in 2000” is an event proposition, not a `$7.5bn` cost proposition;
4. a technology-risk assessment would need a separately defined outcome such as failure counts, service interruption time, critical-system availability, or another pre-specified metric;
5. only after cost and outcome definitions are separately frozen and aligned could a result be judged—post hoc “crisis avoidance” cannot replace the cost outcome.

The case therefore supports one recording rule: **the scale of preventive spending must not be rewritten as the scale of the loss prevented.**

### 3.2 Dow 36,000: the target value is visible, but the timing condition is not

The Internet Archive catalog record confirms the authors, title, 1999 copyright / original edition, and 2000 edition. The title clearly contains the `Dow 36,000` target. FRED’s DJIA series can provide a daily closing-index outcome measure.

The following questions remain open:

- Is 36,000 a momentary touch, a year-end close, an actual total return, or another definition?
- Did the book specify a number of years, a calendar year, or no deadline?
- Is the target a nominal index point or does it imply inflation / dividend assumptions?
- Should the result use first touch, window-end value, or maximum within the window?

Until the book text is recovered, one cannot infer from a later day above 36,000 that the forecast was on time; nor can the absence of a frozen deadline be converted into a miss.

### 3.3 Concorde: the existence of forecast discussion is confirmed, but the forecast numbers are not

The 3 April 1974 House of Lords Hansard records Lord Trefgarne asking British Airways to publish more detailed “forecast losses” from Concorde operations. Lord Beswick replied that the government was discussing how to provide Parliament with more detail about the effect of introducing Concorde services on profitability. The debate also shows that industrial spillovers were a separate requested outcome from direct operating losses.

The record cannot support a numeric judgment because it lacks:

- forecast amount;
- forecast fiscal period or cumulative horizon;
- fare, passenger, load-factor, and route assumptions;
- operating profit, lifecycle cost, or industrial-benefit outcome;
- a boundary between Concorde flights, British Airways as a whole, and national industrial benefit.

Thus “the aircraft later retired” is not an outcome field, and “the project was expensive” is not a forecast error. The original numbers and denominator must be recovered first.

## 4. What this set supports, and what it does not

### Supports

1. **The shared cross-domain problem is field pairing, not a shortage of stories.** Each case has a traceable historical target or forecast discussion, but each exposes a different missing load-bearing field.
2. **Public budgets, market indices, and project profitability cannot share one “success / failure” label.** Each requires its own object, unit, denominator, and window.
3. **“A forecast existed” and “a forecast is judgeable” are different claims.** Y2K lacks a same-basis final cost, Dow lacks timing, and Concorde lacks numeric original and outcome table; all three should stop at unknown.
4. **Historical material can narrow recording rules.** This set reinforces three boundaries: preventive cost is not avoided loss; reaching a value is not reaching it by the deadline; later project retirement is not proof that a commercial forecast was wrong.

### Does not support

- any technology, political, or commercial forecast-accuracy rate;
- counting the three records in the current qualified `CALIBRATION` total;
- a claim that the method is validated, five-gate accuracy improved, or a gate was falsified;
- a claim that historical forecasts generally fail;
- a new future judgment, opportunity candidate, or society-level trend conclusion.
