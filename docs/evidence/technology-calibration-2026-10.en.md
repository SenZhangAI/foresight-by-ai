# Technology-history calibration: evidence boundaries for four common candidates (October 2026)

[中文版](technology-calibration-2026-10.md)

[Back to Retrospect](../en/01-retrospect.md) · [Back to Foresight Methodology](../en/00-method.md)

> **This page records evidence boundaries; it is not a new forecast.** This review examined four candidates often used to prove that “technology forecasts fail”: nuclear power, Carter-era solar, Japan's Fifth Generation Computer Systems project, and consumer virtual reality in the 1990s. Historical material exists for all four, but none closes all of “the original pre-T forecast, a defined target, the same outcome metric, the same denominator, and a fixed observation window.” All four are therefore coded `UNKNOWN/UNVERIFIED`; **none may be counted as a definite technology miss, a qualified `CALIBRATION` pair, or an accuracy denominator**.
>
> This is not a defence of technology forecasting. It decomposes “sounds like a miss” into attackable fields: what was forecast, by when, for whom, in what unit, and whether the outcome artifact concerns the same object. Only after those fields close may a case be coded `MISS`, `PASS`, or `CALIBRATION`.

## 1. Shared coding rule

| Field | Requirement on this page |
|---|---|
| `T` | One time point at which the forecaster made an identifiable claim; all gates use the same `T` |
| Forecast object | Technology, project, product, and social adoption must be separated; a broad “change the world” phrase is not a substitute |
| Target metric | A measurable event, cost, performance, shipment, adoption rate, or project milestone |
| Denominator and unit | Population, devices, projects, currency basis, energy unit, and so on must be definable at `T` and match the outcome |
| Observation window | Fixed in advance; this page audits pairability and does not treat a convenient cut-off as an outcome |
| Outcome material | Must point to the same forecast object; an adjacent technology, replacement project, or retrospective narrative is not automatically an outcome |
| Evidence label | `CALIBRATION` is reserved for a reconstructible, field-complete pair after the outcome is known; one missing field means `UNKNOWN/UNVERIFIED` |

The shared problem is not “there is no material that looks like an outcome.” It is that project history, technology history, industry history, and social adoption history are being collapsed into one metric. A project ending on schedule is not technology failure; a demonstration device being removed from one building is not a zero adoption rate; a product being cancelled is not a zero value for an entire category.

## 3. Case cards

### 3.1 Nuclear power: fix the system boundary before judging a price forecast

**Locatable material.** The IAEA's *A New Look at Nuclear Power Costs* discusses rising nuclear costs and engineering and construction components. Lovering, Yip, and Nordhaus's global historical study assembles reactor-level `overnight construction cost` series for 349 reactors (see the [source table](#5-sources-and-confidence)). These materials establish that nuclear costs can be measured historically; they do not by themselves establish a particular “cheap” or “too cheap to meter” forecast.

**Why this cannot be coded `MISS`.** “Nuclear power will be cheap” may mean marginal generation cost, levelized lifecycle cost, delivered electricity price, overnight construction cost, or total cost including finance and insurance, in one country or period. The study's `overnight construction cost` is not all of those metrics. Mixing metrics, countries, and decades manufactures an apparent miss rather than measuring one.

**Gap and next action.** First locate the original forecast or an auditable pre-T quotation; freeze the unit, currency basis, system boundary, region, and observation window; then judge the result against an official or peer-reviewed series with the same boundary. If the original leaves only a slogan and no denominator, the case remains `UNKNOWN/UNVERIFIED`; a cost-rise narrative cannot be upgraded into miss evidence.

**Strongest alternative explanation.** The forecast object may have been silently substituted: technical potential, unit operating cost, and final project delivery cost were collapsed into one retrospective story. Institutions, finance, licensing, and construction cycles may have changed the system boundary; that is an adoption-and-delivery problem and does not automatically refute a claim about a narrower physical quantity.

### 3.2 Carter-era solar: a demonstration's political fate is not an adoption rate

**Locatable material.** The Smithsonian National Museum of American History records that the Carter administration installed 32 solar panels on the West Wing in 1979 to heat water; they operated for about seven years and were removed in 1986 under the Reagan administration. The White House Historical Association supplies a compatible timeline and political context (see the [source table](#5-sources-and-confidence)).

**Why this cannot be coded `MISS`.** These materials describe the installation and removal of one political demonstration, not a pre-T forecast that “solar” would reach a specified share, cost, or installed capacity by a specified date. The material points to solar hot-water panels; treating it as photovoltaic power and using it as a proxy for social solar adoption crosses a technology boundary, a target denominator, and an outcome metric. Removal may reflect a change of administration, building maintenance, demonstration policy, or equipment life; it cannot by itself identify why a technology forecast was wrong.

**Gap and next action.** Find the original Carter-era energy target, its applicable technology, region, time window, and denominator—household hot water, electricity generation, or installed capacity—and pair it with same-basis energy statistics or policy completion records. If the target was only “a government solar demonstration,” code it as project / policy history, not as a society-level technology forecast.

**Strongest alternative explanation.** This is better suited to calibrating the boundary between political signalling and technology diffusion: a demonstration can be short-lived, and policy symbolism can be reversed while diffusion follows costs, supply chains, permitting, and installation capacity. That explanation still needs independent cases and is not promoted to a new lens here.

### 3.3 Fifth Generation Computer: a research programme's goal cannot be rewritten as a social-product promise

**Locatable material.** The Institute for Processing Society (IPSJ) Computer Museum records ICOT as the central organisation for the Fifth Generation Computer Project and describes prototypes of parallel inference computers. The AIST/ICOT ten-year project report says the project began in 1982, used parallel processing and inference processing based on logic programming, and summarised its results in 1992 (see the [source table](#5-sources-and-confidence)).

**Why this cannot be coded `MISS`.** “Fifth generation” may refer to a research programme's technical goal, a national industrial-policy aspiration, a logic-programming paradigm, or a later media rewrite that promised human-like general intelligent computers in the 1990s. These are not one forecast object. A project completing its research cycle without producing a mass-market product does not by itself show that its technical target was missed; prototype papers or tools do not show society-wide diffusion either.

**Gap and next action.** Extract measurable variables from the original project goals: inference throughput, problem classes, software ecosystem, prototype performance, commercialisation date, or target-user share. Assign an outcome artifact to each variable. If only the name “fifth generation” and a project end date remain, the case shows that planning language existed; it does not show that one unified forecast was falsified.

**Strongest alternative explanation.** The outcome may be a shift in research objectives, a change of technical route, or knowledge and talent spillovers rather than the simple absence of a mass product. Project history can calibrate the boundary between research programmes, national industrial policy, and product diffusion, but “the expected product did not appear” cannot automatically be blamed on basic technical impossibility.

### 3.4 1990s virtual reality: one cancelled product cannot replace a category denominator

**Locatable material.** Public historical records for Sega VR show a headset demonstrated and planned as a home-console peripheral in the early 1990s, but never released to the public. The Video Game History Foundation preserves the project's history and restoration work. The Computer History Museum's XR exhibit places VR, AR, and MR in a cross-decade device and technology lineage rather than treating one product as the category (see the [source table](#5-sources-and-confidence)).

**Why this cannot be coded `MISS`.** “VR will be mainstream in the 1990s” is not scorable until the forecaster, `T`, target country / population, adoption threshold, experience metric, and observation window are specified. Sega VR's non-release may be a product-development or safety / experience threshold outcome; it does not mean all VR products, arcades, professional training, research equipment, or consumer adoption were zero. Conversely, the existence of VR devices today cannot be backfilled into a “1990s forecast hit.”

**Gap and next action.** Start with an enumerated set of period advertisements, company announcements, industry reports, or investment materials, rather than selecting one retrospective slogan. For each forecast record `T`, price / performance, target users, sales or adoption threshold, and then reveal results under one observation window. A cancelled product may enter a product-level failure set, but not a society-level technology-miss denominator by itself.

**Strongest alternative explanation.** Diffusion may have been jointly limited by displays, latency, motion sickness, content supply, price, wearing burden, and social setting. These constraints can explain “the capability exists but the mass form did not emerge,” but this page does not pre-register them as a general rule.

## 4. What this round actually narrows

1. **`UNKNOWN/UNVERIFIED` is not a soft failure.** All four cases retain traceable material, but an incomplete pair must remain unknown; a source's existence is not a pairable outcome.
2. **Forecast object, project object, technology category, and social-adoption object may not be exchanged.** One exchange can turn “project ended” into “technology failed,” or “demonstration removed” into “no society adopted it.”
3. **One metric must run from `T` through the outcome.** Cost, price, capacity, shipments, performance, project milestones, and adoption rates have different denominators; a convenient matching metric may not be selected after the fact.
4. **These four cases add no lens.** They do not satisfy the rule-calibration threshold of at least two successes, two failures, and the ability to say no; the page narrows evidence coding rather than proposing “technology forecasts usually fail.”
5. **The technology-domain qualified count does not change.** As of this record all four cases are `UNKNOWN/UNVERIFIED`; they do not upgrade the technology domain from zero qualified pairs to four, and they do not change the existing whole-repository `6/12` basis.
6. **This page is not a full independent re-review and does not trigger one by itself.** This round adds no lens, scoring rule, diffusion gate, or future judgment-card verdict. If a later round closes any case as `MISS` / `PASS`, or changes a rule on its basis, it must first meet methodology §1.3's complete-scope freeze, de-labelling repair, isolated-packet, and per-language field-check prerequisites. Until then, this page must not be treated as a full re-review or evidence that the method is valid.
